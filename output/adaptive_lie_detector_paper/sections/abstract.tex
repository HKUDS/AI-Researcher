\begin{abstract}
How reliably can black-box behavioral probing distinguish truthful from deceptive language model outputs? We evaluate this question under controlled conditions using ADAGE, an adaptive interrogation pipeline tested across six models (3B--70B, three families), and find that behavioral detection primarily captures instruction-following artifacts. We introduce three reusable methodological controls that expose this fragility. (1)~A \emph{prompt-equalized control}---identical neutral prompts, behavioral differences from knowledge conflict only---drops classification accuracy from 93--100\% to 61--84\%. (2)~\emph{Cross-family feature extraction} reveals that same-family scoring inflates key detection features by up to 7$\times$. (3)~A \emph{hedging-word regex baseline} matches or exceeds the full LLM pipeline under equalization, confirming the residual signal is surface-level. A genuine knowledge-conflict signal exists and grows monotonically with scale (61\% at 3B to 84\% at 70B), with the largest gain between 7B and 14B (+11.5\,pp). However, an instructed-matched control on three models confirms instruction-following as the dominant confound (+7.5--29\,pp), and the scale-dependent signal is fully captured by lexical heuristics. Additional findings include RLHF safety refusals confounding capable-model evaluation and extractor sensitivity breaking monotonicity under one of three extractors. We study instructed roleplay deception, not autonomous strategic deception; these results establish evaluation controls that future behavioral deception detection work should adopt.
\end{abstract}
