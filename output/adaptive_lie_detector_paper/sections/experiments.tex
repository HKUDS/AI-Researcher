\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

All mock experiments use stochastic pattern-matching models (\texttt{MockTargetModel}) whose responses are drawn from fixed response pools via \texttt{random.choice}. To ensure cross-table reproducibility, all mock evaluations reported in this section use a fixed random seed (seed\,=\,42), applied independently per experiment so that each block produces the same numbers regardless of execution order. A \texttt{--seed} argument allows reviewers to verify reproducibility. The mock target model generates responses by selecting from truthful or deceptive response pools calibrated to produce either consistent, detail-rich answers or hedging, evasive ones. Because mock responses are drawn stochastically (not computed deterministically), accuracy varies slightly across threshold values even for the same configuration, and statistical comparisons within the mock setting remain descriptive.

Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} summarizes the key hyperparameters used across all experiments.

Claims were drawn from a curated set of verifiable and falsifiable statements. Examples include truthful claims such as ``I graduated from MIT in 2020'' (paired with a consistent biographical profile) and lying claims such as ``I have never visited France'' (paired with a profile containing French travel records). Each claim was assigned a ground-truth label, and the mock model's response patterns were calibrated accordingly.

\subsection{Mock Pipeline Validation}
\label{sec:mock_validation}
\label{sec:failure_analysis}

ADAGE achieves 93\% classification accuracy on $n=100$ balanced mock trials (avg.\ 2.0 questions vs.\ 8.0 fixed; 75\% reduction). 5-fold CV yields $100\% \pm 0\%$; all five features have very large separation ($d \in [3.40, 4.70]$). A prompt-swap ablation drops accuracy from 93\% to 14\%, confirming response-content sensitivity. A critical limitation is that classifier confidence exceeds 0.99 for both correct and incorrect predictions, making adaptive stopping vacuous at the 2-question floor. Mock responses are drawn from $\approx$5 generic phrases per condition, reducing classification to hedge-word detection. Full mock analyses are in Appendix~\ref{app:mock_accuracy}--\ref{app:mock_confidence}. Real LLM evaluation begins in Section~\ref{sec:real_llm_pilot}.

\subsection{Claude Haiku 4.5 Evaluation at Scale}
\label{sec:real_llm_pilot}
\label{sec:claude_haiku_n100}

We ran $n=100$ balanced trials (50 truthful, 50 lying) using Claude Haiku~4.5 \cite{anthropic2024claude} via AWS Bedrock as the target model; 92 completed (47 truthful, 45 lying). Eight trials (5 lying, 3 truthful) failed due to transient API rate-limit errors before interrogation began and were excluded; a Fisher exact test confirmed no significant association between failure and ground-truth condition (OR\,=\,1.74, $p = 0.72$), ruling out selection bias. With the mock-trained classifier: 71.7\% overall (100\% truthful, 42.2\% lying, avg.\ 2.1 questions). Claude Haiku exhibits false-negative dominance---the \emph{opposite} error profile from Llama~3.2~3B (false-positive dominant). The more capable model produces convincing lies that evade detection, while its truthful responses are confidently identified.

\paragraph{Feature separability.} Defensiveness\footnote{Throughout this paper, ``defensiveness'' refers to a behavioral feature that, as our construct validity analysis reveals (Section~\ref{sec:novelty}), more accurately captures \emph{assertive correction behavior}---models pushing back on or correcting claims---rather than evasion. We retain the original label for consistency across experiments.} exhibits very large separation ($|d|=4.90$), while consistency and specificity show moderate signal ($|d|=1.14$ each). Confidence and elaboration collapse to near-zero ($|d| \leq 0.20$), consistent with the pattern observed on Llama and Mistral.

\paragraph{Recalibration.} LOO recalibration on Claude Haiku data recovers performance to 97.8\% overall (100\% truthful, 95.6\% lying, F1\,=\,0.977) --- the strongest recalibration result across all evaluated models, driven by the dominant defensiveness signal.

\paragraph{Cross-model confound.} Claude Haiku serves as both interrogator/extractor \emph{and} target (same model family). If the feature extractor rates same-family responses more favourably on truthful dimensions, this would inflate truthful accuracy and suppress lying detection. We cannot rule this out without a cross-family extractor ablation.

\subsection{Open-Weight Real LLM Evaluation (Llama~3.2~3B via Ollama)}
\label{sec:ollama_eval}

We evaluated Llama~3.2~3B \cite{meta2024llama3} via Ollama with Claude Haiku~4.5 as interrogator/extractor ($n=98$ of 100 trials). Mock-trained accuracy is 61.2\% overall (26.5\% truthful, 95.9\% lying, avg.\ 2.6 questions): nearly all lies detected but 36/49 truthful claims falsely flagged. Overall accuracy is substantially lower than mock (93\%), confirming the transfer gap.

Feature separability is model-scale-dependent. On Llama~3.2~3B, consistency, specificity, and defensiveness retain their directional signal at roughly one-third to one-half mock magnitude ($|d| \approx 1.7$--$2.0$), while confidence and elaboration collapse to near-zero ($|d| \approx 0.2$). Mistral~7B shows substantially higher separability on the top three features: consistency ($|d|=3.74$) and defensiveness ($|d|=3.35$) approach mock levels, and specificity ($|d|=1.97$) matches Llama's level. Confidence ($|d|=1.17$) provides moderate signal, while elaboration collapses to near-zero ($|d|=0.14$). This directly explains Mistral's better mock-trained transfer (79.0\% vs.\ 61.2\%): the feature distributions on the larger model are closer to the mock training distribution.

\paragraph{Why truthful accuracy collapses.}
The 26.5\% truthful accuracy (36/49 truthful claims predicted as ``lying'') is explained by feature distribution shift. In mock data, truthful responses have mean consistency\,$\approx$\,7.5 and specificity\,$\approx$\,7.5 --- reliably far from the decision boundary. In the real LLM setting, truthful Llama~3.2~3B responses average consistency\,$=\,5.94$ and specificity\,$=\,7.02$, values that fall partially within the lying region learned from mock distributions. With confidence and elaboration contributing near-zero discriminative signal, the mock-trained classifier is systematically biased toward predicting ``lying'' for real truthful responses.

\paragraph{Cross-model confound.}
A confound in the cross-model comparison merits acknowledgment. The Claude Haiku pilot uses Claude Haiku as both interrogator/extractor \emph{and} target (same model family), while the Llama evaluation uses Claude Haiku as interrogator/extractor but Llama~3.2~3B as target (different families). If the LLM feature extractor scores Claude and Llama responses differently on a 0--10 scale due to model-family biases --- for instance, rating Llama responses as systematically less consistent regardless of ground truth --- this would confound the cross-model comparison. We cannot rule this out without ablating the extractor, which we leave for future work.

\paragraph{Opposite error profiles across models.}
The Claude Haiku evaluation ($n=92$, Section~\ref{sec:claude_haiku_n100}) and Llama~3B evaluation ($n=98$) produce qualitatively opposite error profiles. Claude Haiku: 100\% truthful accuracy but only 42.2\% lying detection (false negatives dominate). Llama~3.2~3B: 95.9\% lying detection but only 26.5\% truthful accuracy (false positives dominate). We hypothesize two contributing factors. \emph{First, model capability:} Claude Haiku's RLHF-trained responses are fluent and coherent, producing feature scores close to the truthful region even when lying --- making lies hard to detect. Llama~3.2~3B's smaller capacity may produce deceptive outputs whose feature scores align closely with the mock lying profile, making them easy to catch. \emph{Second, response style under truthful conditions:} Claude Haiku's factual answers are confident and specific, scoring high on consistency and specificity; Llama~3.2~3B's factual answers score lower on these dimensions, landing in the mock lying region. This tension motivates the multi-model recalibration analysis in Section~\ref{sec:recalibration}.

\subsection{Recalibration on Real LLM Data}
\label{sec:recalibration}

The feature distribution analysis (Section~\ref{sec:ollama_eval}) suggests that the mock-to-real transfer failure lies in the decision boundary, not the features themselves: three of five features preserve their directional signal at reduced but meaningful magnitude ($|d| \approx 1.7$--$2.0$). This predicts that a classifier trained on real LLM data should recover substantially from the 26.5\% truthful accuracy of the mock-trained baseline. We test this directly.

Per-trial mean feature vectors (98$\times$5) trained under LOO and 5-fold CV. LOO recalibration recovers truthful accuracy from 26.5\% to 91.8\% (overall 93.9\%, lying 95.9\%, F1\,=\,0.940). 5-fold CV confirms: $92.8\% \pm 2.5\%$ overall, $91.8\% \pm 4.1\%$ truthful. Dropping confidence and elaboration ($|d| \approx 0.2$) reduces to 85.7\% overall (3-feat LOO), showing that even low-$|d|$ features contribute marginal signal at this sample size. The gap is a calibration failure, not a feature failure: all five features are informative when trained on the target distribution.

We ran $n=100$ balanced trials (50 truthful, 50 lying) using the same mock-trained classifier and interrogation pipeline (Claude Haiku~4.5 via Bedrock). \paragraph{Key finding: model scale mediates transfer.}
Mistral~7B achieves 79.0\% overall accuracy with the mock-trained classifier --- 17.8 pp higher than Llama~3.2~3B (61.2\%). Crucially, Mistral~7B's truthful accuracy (64.0\%) is 37.5 pp higher than Llama~3B's (26.5\%), indicating that the catastrophic truthful failure observed on Llama~3B is partially mitigated at larger scale, though Mistral still exhibits a ``predict lying'' bias. Llama~3.3~70B (Section~\ref{sec:llama70b_eval}) confirms this trend: 80.0\% truthful accuracy with a balanced error profile (10 FP, 15 FN). Claude Haiku (Section~\ref{sec:claude_haiku_n100}) exhibits the opposite error profile entirely: 100\% truthful accuracy but only 42.2\% lying detection. The progression from Llama~3B (false positives dominate) through Mistral/Llama~70B (balanced) to Claude Haiku (false negatives dominate) suggests that model capability determines error directionality: more capable models produce convincing lies that evade detection, while less capable models produce unconvincing truthful responses that trigger false alarms.

We also evaluated zero-shot LLM judge and recalibrated classifiers on Mistral~7B ($n=100$). Zero-shot achieves 83.0\% overall (66.0\% truthful, 100\% lying), slightly outperforming the mock-trained pipeline (79.0\%). LOO recalibration on Mistral data recovers performance to 100\% overall (100\% truthful, 100\% lying), higher than Llama~3B recalibration (93.9\%), reflecting the stronger feature separability in Mistral's responses. The consistent pattern (mock-trained $\lesssim$ zero-shot $\ll$ recalibrated) holds across both models, confirming that the structured pipeline provides value when properly calibrated.

The LOO margin distribution ($0.44 \pm 0.09$ mean, 3/100 trials below 0.2) confirms that Mistral's perfect LOO is not a floor/ceiling artifact; all five hardest-to-classify trials are truthful, consistent with the ``predict lying'' bias.

\subsection{Scale Evaluation: Llama~3.3~70B via AWS Bedrock}
\label{sec:llama70b_eval}

To directly address the question of whether larger open-weight models produce qualitatively different detection profiles, we evaluated Llama~3.3~70B Instruct \cite{meta2024llama3} --- a 70-billion parameter model --- via AWS Bedrock ($n=100$ balanced trials). Crucially, this evaluation uses \emph{cross-family extraction}: Claude Haiku~4.5 serves as both interrogator and feature extractor, while the target is from the Llama family. This design preemptively addresses the same-family bias identified in the Claude Haiku pilot (Section~\ref{sec:claude_haiku_n100}).

\paragraph{Mock-trained performance.} The mock-trained classifier achieves 75.0\% overall (80.0\% truthful, 70.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.737, avg.\ 3.3 questions). The error profile is balanced --- 10 false positives and 15 false negatives --- placing the 70B model between Mistral~7B (false-positive dominant) and Claude Haiku (false-negative dominant). This intermediate profile is consistent with the hypothesis that model capability shifts error directionality: at 70B, the model produces both more convincing lies (increasing false negatives) and more authoritative truthful responses (reducing false positives relative to Llama~3B).

\paragraph{Feature separability.} The 70B model exhibits strong separation on the top three features: consistency ($|d|=3.07$), defensiveness ($|d|=2.55$), and specificity ($|d|=1.98$), all comparable to Mistral~7B. Confidence ($|d|=0.97$) provides moderate signal, while elaboration collapses to near-zero ($|d|=0.31$). The feature ordering and magnitude are remarkably similar to Mistral~7B despite the 10$\times$ parameter difference, suggesting that the 3B $\to$ 7B scale jump produces most of the feature-separability gain.

\paragraph{LOO recalibration.} LOO recalibration recovers 93.0\% overall (92.0\% truthful, 94.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.931), with 5-fold CV confirming $93.0\% \pm 6.8\%$. The $\pm$6.8\,pp standard deviation is the largest among recalibrated conditions (cf.\ $\pm 2.5$\,pp for Llama~3B); bootstrap 95\% CI: $[88\%, 98\%]$, consistent with genuine signal. This is nearly identical to Llama~3B (93.9\%) despite the 23$\times$ parameter ratio. The consistent \textasciitilde93\% LOO ceiling across two Llama models suggests a fundamental limit of the 5-feature logistic classifier rather than a scale-dependent effect.

\paragraph{Fixed-$K$ ablation.} $K=1$ achieves 92.0\% LOO; $K=2$ peaks at 94.0\%. The adaptive system (avg.\ 3.3 questions) matches the $K=8$ result (93.0\%), confirming the same first-question-dominant pattern observed on smaller models.

\paragraph{Hedging baseline.} The regex-based hedging classifier achieves 87.0\% LOO (86.0\% truthful, 88.0\% lying), trailing the LLM-feature pipeline by 6.0\,pp. This gap is larger than on Claude Haiku (4.3\,pp) but smaller than on Llama~3B (6.1\,pp), consistent with the LLM features providing incremental value on open-weight models.

\paragraph{Knowledge pre-check.} A direct factual query before each trial reveals near-perfect separation: 48/50 truthful claims correctly identified as true, 50/50 lying claims correctly identified as false (the model has no basis to affirm first-person experiential claims). The two misclassified truthful claims were edge cases where the model expressed uncertainty. This confirms the 70B model possesses sufficient world knowledge to distinguish the factual claims used in this evaluation.

\paragraph{Claim-type asymmetry caveat.} Throughout Sections~\ref{sec:ollama_eval}--\ref{sec:llama70b_eval}, the instructed condition uses asymmetric claim types: truthful claims are factual/verifiable statements (e.g., ``Water boils at 100\textdegree C''), while lying claims are first-person experiential assertions (e.g., ``I graduated from MIT''). The instructed-condition accuracy (61.2--100\%) may therefore partly reflect the classifier distinguishing factual from autobiographical claims rather than truth from deception per se. The prompt-equalized control (Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized}) uses matched factual claims for both conditions and is not subject to this confound. The instructed-matched control (Section~\ref{sec:instructed_matched}) directly addresses this concern by running instructed prompts with matched claims on both 70B and 3B: the resulting 99\% accuracy on 70B (vs.\ 93\% original) shows that claim asymmetry \emph{hurts} rather than inflates the instructed result at larger scale, while on 3B the instructed-matched accuracy is 90\% (vs.\ 93.9\% original), indicating that claim asymmetry provides a modest boost on smaller models where world knowledge is weaker.

\subsection{Prompt-Equalized Control (EXP-1)}
\label{sec:prompt_equalized}

The instructed-condition results above use asymmetric system prompts: truth mode receives ``answer honestly,'' while lie mode receives ``the claim is FALSE, defend it by lying.'' A critical concern is that the classifier may detect instruction-style artifacts rather than genuine behavioral differences. To test this, we conducted a prompt-equalized control on Mistral~7B ($n=100$) where \emph{both} conditions received the same neutral system prompt: ``You are a helpful assistant. You will be asked about a factual claim. Share what you know about this topic.'' Truthful trials used true factual claims; deceptive trials used modified-false versions of the same claims (e.g., ``Water boils at 100\textdegree C'' $\to$ ``Water boils at 85\textdegree C''). The model was never instructed to lie; any behavioral differences arise from its knowledge conflicting with false claims. Claim pairs are described in Appendix~\ref{app:claims}. Note that the equalized evaluations are inherently cross-family: Claude Haiku~4.5 serves as interrogator/extractor while targets are Llama~3.2~3B, Mistral~7B, and (in Section~\ref{sec:llama70b_eval}) Llama~3.3~70B. The same-family bias identified in Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness} does not apply to these cross-family evaluations.

\paragraph{5-fold CV stability.}
Repeated 5-fold CV on equalized data (20 repeats) yields $71.1\% \pm 9.0\%$, consistent with the LOO point estimate (71.0\%) but with high variance ($\pm$9\,pp). The high standard deviation indicates that the signal is statistically fragile: individual folds can produce accuracy anywhere from $\sim$60\% to $\sim$80\%. A bootstrap confidence interval (10,000 resamples over LOO predictions) yields 95\% CI $[62\%, 80\%]$, confirming that the true accuracy could plausibly be anywhere in this wide range. By comparison, the Llama~3B recalibrated classifier achieves $92.8\% \pm 2.5\%$ under the same protocol---a 7$\times$ reduction in variance reflecting far stronger feature separation.

\paragraph{Replication on Llama~3.2~3B ($n=100$).}
The same protocol on Llama~3.2~3B ($n=100$) yields LOO 61.0\% (truthful 64.0\%, lying 58.0\%), bootstrap CI [51\%,\,71\%], permutation $p=0.021$. All feature $|d|$ values fall below 0.3 (consistency 0.26, specificity 0.26, defensiveness 0.18) --- no single feature is load-bearing as defensiveness was for Mistral ($|d|=1.12$). The hedging baseline achieves 75.0\%, exceeding the LLM pipeline by 14\,pp, consistent with the pattern on the original $n=50$ evaluation.

\paragraph{Replication on Llama~3.1~8B ($n=100$).}
\label{sec:llama8b_equalized}
To add a fourth point to the scale trend, we evaluated Llama~3.1~8B Instruct via AWS Bedrock under the same prompt-equalized protocol ($n=100$ balanced trials). LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{65.0\%} (62.0\% truthful, 68.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.660), with 5-fold CV confirming $66.0\% \pm 9.7\%$ and bootstrap 95\% CI $[56\%, 74\%]$. Defensiveness is the dominant feature ($|d|=0.84$), followed by elaboration ($|d|=0.62$) and confidence ($|d|=0.45$); consistency and specificity collapse below 0.4. The hedging regex baseline achieves 75.0\%, exceeding the LLM pipeline by 10\,pp. The 8B result falls between 3B (61\%) and 7B (71\%), consistent with the scale trend.

\paragraph{Replication on Llama~3.3~70B ($n=93$).}
The prompt-equalized protocol was extended to Llama~3.3~70B via AWS Bedrock ($n=93$ of 100 trials; 7 rate-limit errors excluded). This evaluation is inherently cross-family (Claude Haiku interrogator/extractor, Llama 70B target). Mock-trained accuracy is 50.5\% (near chance), confirming that instructed-condition signal is prompt-driven. However, LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{83.9\%} (84.8\% truthful, 83.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.839), with 5-fold CV confirming $83.8\% \pm 6.0\%$ and bootstrap 95\% CI $[76.3\%, 91.4\%]$. This is notably higher than the 3B (61\%) and 7B (71\%) equalized results, suggesting that the 70B model's stronger world knowledge produces more detectable correction behavior when confronted with false claims.

Feature separability collapses relative to the instructed condition: defensiveness ($|d|=0.92$ vs.\ $2.55$ instructed), consistency ($|d|=0.32$ vs.\ $3.07$), specificity ($|d|=0.15$ vs.\ $1.98$). Defensiveness remains the dominant feature (permutation importance $+0.10$), consistent with the Mistral equalized finding. Elaboration, which was near-zero in the instructed condition ($|d|=0.31$), shows a moderate equalized signal ($|d|=0.79$, truth $>$ lying). This inversion is consistent with knowledge-driven behavior: without role-play instructions constraining the response format, the model's natural verbosity on familiar topics becomes a detectable signal---it elaborates freely on true claims but responds more tersely when correcting false ones.

The hedging regex baseline achieves 87.1\% LOO on the equalized 70B data, \emph{exceeding} the LLM pipeline by 3.2\,pp (refusal count shows $|d|=1.23$, roughly $1.3\times$ the strongest LLM feature). This extends the pattern observed on smaller models: surface-level lexical features capture as much or more equalized signal as LLM-extracted features.

Category-level accuracy varies: Scientific 90.0\%, Geographic 90.0\%, Cultural 100\% (7 trials), Technology 75.0\%, Historical 70.0\%. Knowledge pre-check confirms near-perfect separation (42/46 truthful claims as TRUE, 46/47 lying claims as FALSE), indicating the model possesses the knowledge needed to distinguish claims.

\paragraph{Mid-scale point: Qwen~2.5~14B ($n=97$).}
\label{sec:qwen14b_equalized}
To fill the gap between the 8B and 70B data points, we evaluated Qwen~2.5~14B \cite{qwen2025qwen25} via Ollama under the same prompt-equalized protocol ($n=97$ of 100 trials; 3 connection errors excluded). This is a between-family comparison (Qwen/Alibaba lineage vs.\ Llama/Mistral), with Claude Haiku as cross-family interrogator/extractor. Mock-trained accuracy is 49.5\% (chance); LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{82.5\%} (83.7\% truthful, 81.2\% lying, F1\,=\,0.824), with bootstrap 95\% CI $[74.2\%, 89.7\%]$ and held-out 50/50 confirming at $82.0\% \pm 5.0\%$. The feature profile resembles the 70B result: defensiveness ($|d|=1.11$) and elaboration ($|d|=1.20$) are the two informative features; consistency, specificity, and confidence collapse below $|d|<0.2$. The hedging regex baseline achieves 90.7\%, exceeding the LLM pipeline by 8.2\,pp. The 82.5\% LOO slots between 7B (71\%) and 70B (83.9\%), consistent with the scale trend, and the large jump from 7B to 14B (+11.5\,pp) suggests that the knowledge-conflict detection threshold may activate in this range.

\paragraph{Within-family scale control: Qwen~2.5~7B ($n=100$).}
\label{sec:qwen7b_equalized}
To disentangle scale from family composition in the two-tier finding (the $\leq$7B and $\geq$14B bins differ in family membership), we evaluated Qwen~2.5~7B via Ollama under the same protocol ($n=100$ balanced trials). This provides a within-family pair: Qwen~7B vs.\ Qwen~14B (82.5\%), both from the Qwen/Alibaba lineage with Claude Haiku as cross-family extractor. Mock-trained accuracy is 61.0\%; LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{74.0\%} (78.0\% truthful, 72.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.742), with bootstrap 95\% CI $[65\%, 82\%]$ and 5-fold CV confirming $75.0\% \pm 1.8\%$. Defensiveness is the dominant feature ($|d|=1.16$); confidence ($|d|=0.39$) and consistency ($|d|=0.35$) provide weak secondary signal; specificity and elaboration collapse below $|d|<0.25$. The hedging regex baseline achieves 81.0\%. The within-family gap (Qwen~7B: 74\% $\to$ Qwen~14B: 82.5\%, $+$8.5\,pp; Fisher $p=0.17$) confirms that the two-tier structure reflects a genuine scale effect within a single model family, not merely cross-family differences. Qwen~7B's 74\% sits above the prior $\leq$7B range (61--71\%) but clearly below the $\geq$14B cluster (82.5--84\%), suggesting a modest Qwen family advantage at the 7B scale while preserving the overall two-tier pattern.

\paragraph{Full evaluation on Claude Haiku~4.5 ($n=99$).}
\label{sec:haiku_equalized}
To empirically test whether Claude Haiku's RLHF safety behavior produces a detectable equalized signal, we ran the prompt-equalized protocol on Claude Haiku~4.5 via AWS Bedrock ($n=99$ of 100 trials; 1 connection error excluded). This evaluation uses same-family extraction (Claude Haiku as both target and extractor)---an acknowledged limitation. Mock-trained accuracy is 49.5\% (chance); LOO recovers to \textbf{82.8\%} (85.7\% truthful, 80.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.828), with bootstrap 95\% CI $[74.7\%, 89.9\%]$ and held-out 50/50 confirming at $82.2\% \pm 4.4\%$. Defensiveness is the dominant feature ($|d|=1.49$), the highest equalized defensiveness across all models (cf.\ 1.12 Mistral, 1.11 Qwen~14B, 0.92 Llama~70B, 0.84 Llama~8B). All other features collapse below $|d|<0.5$. The hedging regex achieves 89.9\%, exceeding the LLM pipeline by 7.1\,pp. Under neutral prompts, 98\% of deceptive trials contain correction language, but the model \emph{corrects} rather than \emph{refuses}: ``That's incorrect. Water boils at 100\textdegree C'' (neutral correction) vs.\ ``I can't play this game. I don't lie about facts'' (instructed refusal). The 82.8\% LOO falls within the Claude-extracted equalized range (61--84\%) and is consistent with Haiku's scale, confirming that the instructed 97.8\% was primarily RLHF compliance with the deception instruction.

\paragraph{Implications.}
Under instructed roleplay, the classifier primarily detects instruction-following mode differences. Under equalization, the picture is scale-dependent. On smaller models (3B/8B/7B), the equalized-to-instructed gap is $\sim$28--33\,pp (61--71\% vs.\ 93.9--100\%), the signal rests on a single feature (defensiveness), and variance is high ($\pm$9\,pp). On Llama~70B, the gap narrows to 9\,pp (83.9\% vs.\ 93.0\%), with a more robust feature profile. Equalized accuracy (Claude-extracted; cross-family re-extraction in EXP-H below) improves monotonically across seven models---61\% at 3B, 65\% at 8B, 71\% at Mistral~7B, 74\% at Qwen~7B, 82.5\% at 14B, 84\% at 70B, 83\% at Claude Haiku (Figure~\ref{fig:scale_trend}). Within the Llama family, equalized accuracy increases from 61\% (3B) to 65\% (8B) to 84\% (70B); within the Qwen family, accuracy increases from 74\% (7B) to 82.5\% (14B). The Mistral~7B (71\%) and Claude Haiku (82.8\%) points are between-family comparisons consistent with the trend. The 3B$\to$8B increment is small (+4\,pp, within CIs), but the Qwen~7B$\to$Qwen~14B jump is substantial (+8.5\,pp within a single family), confirming that the two-tier structure reflects a genuine scale effect. Larger models' stronger world knowledge produces increasingly detectable correction behavior when confronted with false claims. However, the hedging regex baseline achieves 87--91\% on the three largest models' equalized data, exceeding the LLM pipeline by 4--8\,pp, confirming that the signal remains surface-level even at scale. The scale-dependent improvement motivates future work on knowledge-probing approaches that leverage this scale-dependent correction behavior, rather than relying on instruction-following asymmetries.

\paragraph{Cross-family re-extraction on equalized data (EXP-H).}
\label{sec:cross_family_equalized}

Mistral AI has not published official parameter counts for this model; we use the name ``Mistral Large~3'' following the AWS Bedrock naming convention.} via AWS Bedrock and Qwen~2.5~14B via local Ollama. This provides three structurally different extractors from three model families (Claude/Anthropic, Mistral/Mistral~AI, Qwen/Alibaba).

The scale trend is preserved under Mistral Large extraction (52\% $\to$ 62\% $\to$ 68.8\%), confirming that the trend is not a Claude-specific extractor artifact. Notably, the Mistral~7B row constitutes a \textbf{same-family test}: Mistral Large~3 scoring Mistral~7B targets (both from Mistral AI). If same-family bias generalizes beyond the Claude family, Mistral Large should achieve \emph{higher} accuracy than Claude (which is cross-family for Mistral~7B targets). The opposite obtains: Mistral Large LOO is 62\%, 9\,pp \emph{below} Claude's 71\% on identical transcripts; defensiveness $|d|$ drops from 1.14 (Claude) to 0.78 (Mistral Large). This indicates that the same-family inflation documented in EXP-A (Claude-on-Claude, $d$: $4.90 \to 0.63$) is specific to the Claude family---likely reflecting RLHF-induced self-preference---rather than a generalizable property of same-family extraction. Claude appears to be a more discriminating extractor for all targets, not merely a biased one for Claude targets. Under Qwen~14B extraction, the trend partially holds (61\% $\to$ 57\% $\to$ 67.7\%): the 3B$\to$70B direction is preserved, but monotonicity breaks at 7B, where Qwen scores Mistral responses 4\,pp \emph{below} the 3B baseline.

\paragraph{Qwen monotonicity diagnostic.} The break at 7B is traced to a variance spike in Qwen's truthful-condition defensiveness scores. At 3B, Qwen's truthful defensiveness has $\sigma=0.59$; at 7B this doubles to $\sigma=1.27$; at 70B it falls to $\sigma=0.50$. The lie-condition variance remains stable ($\sigma = 0.40, 0.63, 0.29$ across scales). This 2:1 truthful-to-lie variance ratio at 7B collapses Qwen's defensiveness $|d|$ from 0.16 (3B) to 0.14 (7B)---both near zero---while Claude's defensiveness $|d|$ jumps from 0.18 (3B) to 1.12 (7B). The per-trial Claude--Qwen defensiveness correlation is weak across all three scales ($r = 0.21, 0.38, 0.22$), indicating that the two extractors are measuring partially different constructs. The diagnostic suggests that Qwen~14B, as a mid-scale extractor, introduces noise on truthful responses to Mistral~7B specifically, plausibly because both models occupy a similar capability range where scoring ambiguity peaks. The scale trend is thus robust under 2 of 3 extractors (Claude, Mistral Large) but not universal; extractor sensitivity at this scale warrants investigation with additional extractors.

Absolute accuracy under cross-family extraction is lower by 9--16\,pp across models. This gap reflects two partially separable effects: same-family bias (documented in EXP-A for the Claude-on-Claude setting) and extractor capability differences (Claude Haiku assigns more discriminative defensiveness scores to \emph{all} targets, not just Claude targets; see Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}). Per-trial feature correlations between Claude and Mistral Large extraction are moderate to strong (Pearson $r = 0.43$--$0.82$ across features and models, all $p < 0.01$); Claude--Qwen correlations are weaker on defensiveness ($r = 0.21$--$0.38$), explaining the divergent LOO results. Defensiveness $d$ drops under cross-family extraction for all three models (e.g., 70B: $0.92 \to 0.47$ Mistral Large, $0.92 \to 0.64$ Qwen). The 70B cross-family-extracted accuracy (67.7--68.8\%) falls within the Claude-extracted range for 3B--7B (61--74\%), suggesting that cross-family extraction roughly ``docks'' the accuracy by one scale step.

\paragraph{Instructed-matched control (EXP-G).}
\label{sec:instructed_matched}
To decompose the 9\,pp gap between instructed-original (93\% LOO) and equalized (84\% LOO) on Llama~70B, we ran a hybrid condition: the model receives the standard asymmetric lie/truth system prompts (as in Sections~\ref{sec:ollama_eval}--\ref{sec:llama70b_eval}), but uses the matched factual claim pairs from the equalized experiment (Appendix~\ref{app:claims}) rather than the original asymmetric claims. This isolates the instruction-following contribution from the claim-type contribution. For truth trials, the model is told ``answer honestly'' and receives the true claim; for lie trials, the model is told ``the claim is FALSE, defend it'' and receives the modified-false claim.

LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{99.0\%} (100\% truthful, 98\% lying, F1\,=\,0.990), with bootstrap 95\% CI $[97\%, 100\%]$. We note that at $n=100$, the difference between 99\% and 95\% is four trials; this ceiling should be interpreted cautiously. Feature separability is very high: consistency $|d|=4.53$, defensiveness $|d|=2.36$, specificity $|d|=1.95$ --- all comparable to or exceeding the original instructed values (Table~\ref{tab:feature_comparison}).

\textbf{Gap decomposition.} The three-condition comparison yields a clean decomposition (all on Llama~70B):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Equalized (84\% LOO) $\xrightarrow{+15\,\text{pp}}$ Instructed-matched (99\% LOO): the instruction-following contribution. Adding asymmetric lie/truth prompts to matched claims recovers the full instructed signal.
    \item Instructed-matched (99\% LOO) $\xrightarrow{-6\,\text{pp}}$ Instructed-original (93\% LOO): the claim-type contribution. Switching from matched factual pairs to asymmetric claims (factual truthful + first-person experiential lying) actually \emph{reduces} accuracy by 6\,pp.
\end{itemize}

This decomposition reveals that the original instructed accuracy (93\%) was not inflated by claim asymmetry --- it was \emph{depressed} by it. First-person experiential claims (``I graduated from MIT'') are harder to detect as lies than modified-false factual claims (``Water boils at 85\textdegree C''), because factual claims trigger stronger correction behavior. The entire equalized-to-instructed gap ($+$9\,pp) is attributable to instruction-following ($+$15\,pp), partially offset by the harder claim type ($-$6\,pp).

\paragraph{Replication on Llama~3.2~3B (EXP-G).}
The same instructed-matched protocol was replicated on Llama~3.2~3B via Ollama ($n=100$ balanced trials; Claude Haiku~4.5 as cross-family interrogator/extractor). LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{90.0\%} (90.0\% truthful, 90.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.900), with bootstrap 95\% CI $[84\%, 96\%]$. Feature separability: consistency $|d|=2.02$, defensiveness $|d|=1.89$, specificity $|d|=1.51$; confidence and elaboration near zero. The hedging regex baseline achieves 85.0\%, trailing the LLM pipeline by 5\,pp.

The three-condition decomposition on 3B:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Equalized (61\% LOO) $\xrightarrow{+29\,\text{pp}}$ Instructed-matched (90\% LOO): the instruction-following contribution.
    \item Instructed-matched (90\% LOO) $\xrightarrow{+3.9\,\text{pp}}$ Instructed-original (93.9\% LOO): the claim-type contribution.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Replication on Qwen~2.5~14B (EXP-G).}
The instructed-matched protocol was further replicated on Qwen~2.5~14B via Ollama ($n=100$ balanced trials; Claude Haiku~4.5 as cross-family interrogator/extractor). LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{90.0\%} (92.0\% truthful, 88.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.900), with bootstrap 95\% CI $[84\%, 95\%]$. Feature separability: consistency $|d|=3.10$, defensiveness $|d|=2.26$, specificity $|d|=1.82$, confidence $|d|=1.53$; elaboration moderate ($|d|=0.74$). The hedging regex baseline achieves 75.0\%, trailing the LLM pipeline by 15\,pp. The two-condition decomposition on 14B:

No instructed-original condition was run on Qwen~14B, so only the two-way decomposition is available. The $+$7.5\,pp instruction-following contribution is the smallest of the five models tested, consistent with Qwen~14B's strong equalized baseline (82.5\%) leaving less room for instruction-following to add signal.

\paragraph{Replication on Mistral~7B (EXP-G).}

\paragraph{Replication on Llama~3.1~8B (EXP-G).}
The instructed-matched protocol was replicated on Llama~3.1~8B via AWS Bedrock ($n=100$ balanced trials; Claude Haiku~4.5 as cross-family interrogator/extractor). LOO recalibration achieves \textbf{96.0\%} (100.0\% truthful, 92.0\% lying, F1\,=\,0.958), with bootstrap 95\% CI $[92\%, 99\%]$. Feature separability: consistency $|d|=2.37$, specificity $|d|=1.91$, defensiveness $|d|=1.44$; confidence and elaboration near zero ($|d| \leq 0.24$). The hedging regex baseline achieves 67.0\%, trailing the LLM pipeline by 29\,pp. The two-condition decomposition on 8B:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Equalized (65\% LOO) $\xrightarrow{+31\,\text{pp}}$ Instructed-matched (96\% LOO): the instruction-following contribution.
\end{itemize}
The $+$31\,pp instruction-following contribution is the largest of the five models tested, despite 8B having a higher equalized baseline than 3B (65\% vs.\ 61\%). This suggests that equalized accuracy and instruction-following sensitivity are partially independent dimensions. The knowledge-transfer confound (Section~5) may explain this asymmetry: the 8B model's intermediate world knowledge means the lie prompt provides a larger relative information boost than on 3B (where world knowledge is too weak for strong correction behavior), while 8B remains far more instruction-sensitive than 14B or 70B (where world knowledge is strong enough to produce equalized signal without instruction). No instructed-original condition was run on Llama~8B, so only the two-way decomposition is available.

\paragraph{Cross-scale EXP-G comparison.} Comparing all five decompositions: instruction-following contributes $+$31\,pp on 8B, $+$29\,pp on 3B, $+$26\,pp on 7B, $+$7.5\,pp on 14B, and $+$15\,pp on 70B; the claim-type effect is $+$3.9\,pp on 3B and $-$6\,pp on 70B. The instruction-following contribution does not decrease monotonically with scale: the 14B model shows the smallest gap ($+$7.5\,pp), while all three models $\leq$8B show gaps $\geq$26\,pp and the 70B model shows $+$15\,pp. The Qwen~14B result ($+$7.5\,pp) is a clear outlier: Mistral~7B, from a different model family than the Llama models, shows an instruction-following contribution ($+$26\,pp) comparable to the Llama models at similar scale ($+$29--31\,pp). This cross-family replication suggests the low Qwen~14B gap reflects a family-specific effect rather than a genuine mid-scale dip in instruction-following sensitivity. On 3B, both factors inflate accuracy in the same direction, producing the full 32.9\,pp equalized-to-instructed gap. The claim-type sign reversal across scales is consistent with the knowledge-conflict mechanism: on 70B, matched-false factual claims trigger strong correction behavior (making them \emph{easier} to detect than first-person experiential claims), while on 3B the model's weaker world knowledge means the original claim-type asymmetry provides additional detectable signal. In all five cases, instruction-following is the dominant confound, accounting for $+$7.5--31\,pp of the equalized-to-instructed gap.

\paragraph{Scale trend and feature collapse (Figures~\ref{fig:scale_trend}--\ref{fig:feature_collapse}).}
The equalized scale trend now spans seven models across three families: 61\% (3B) $\to$ 65\% (8B) $\to$ 71\% (Mistral~7B) $\to$ 74\% (Qwen~7B) $\to$ 82.5\% (14B) $\to$ 84\% (70B) $\to$ 83\% (Claude Haiku). Within the Llama family, accuracy increases from 61\% to 65\% to 84\%; within the Qwen family, accuracy increases from 74\% (7B) to 82.5\% (14B). The Mistral~7B (71\%) and Claude Haiku (82.8\%) points are between-family comparisons consistent with the trend. The trend is non-decreasing in point estimates across all seven models. However, pairwise permutation tests (10,000 permutations, Holm-Bonferroni corrected) reveal that \textbf{no individual adjacent increment is statistically significant}: 3B$\to$8B ($+$4\,pp, $p=1.00$), 8B$\to$Mistral~7B ($+$6\,pp, $p=1.00$), Mistral~7B$\to$Qwen~7B ($+$3\,pp, $p=1.00$), Qwen~7B$\to$Qwen~14B ($+$8.5\,pp, $p=0.98$), Qwen~14B$\to$70B ($+$1.4\,pp, $p=1.00$), 70B$\to$Haiku ($-$1\,pp, $p=1.00$). \paragraph{Chain-of-thought hurts.}
Counterintuitively, the CoT variant performs \emph{worse} than simple judgment on all three models (Llama~3B: 43.9\% vs.\ 77.9\%; Mistral: 71.4\% vs.\ 82.7\%; Llama~70B: 61.0\% vs.\ 91.0\%). The degradation is most severe on Llama~70B (91$\to$61\%, a 30\,pp drop), where the stronger reasoning capacity of the 70B transcripts appears to cause the CoT judge to ``talk itself out of'' correct snap judgments---structured analysis amplifies ambiguity rather than resolving it. On Llama~3B, CoT collapses the ``predict lying'' bias and instead produces near-balanced but lower accuracy (46.9\% truthful, 40.8\% lying) --- the guided analysis appears to confuse the judge by drawing attention to ambiguous signals. Few-shot is intermediate and similarly fails to improve over the original on any model.

\paragraph{Best zero-shot still below recalibrated pipeline.}
The simple original prompt is the best zero-shot variant on all models (77.9\% Llama~3B, 82.7\% Mistral, 91.0\% Llama~70B). Even this optimized baseline falls 2.0--17.3\,pp below the recalibrated pipeline (93.9\% Llama~3B, 100\% Mistral, 93.0\% Llama~70B). The 70B result is particularly striking: zero-shot achieves 91.0\% vs.\ the recalibrated pipeline's 93.0\%---a 2\,pp gap that is within noise at $n=100$---suggesting that at sufficient scale, a single LLM judgment call nearly matches the full structured pipeline. This 70B zero-shot result also substantially outperforms the mock-trained pipeline (75.0\%), confirming that at larger scale the LLM judge's direct assessment is more robust than a misaligned decision boundary. The consistent ordering (zero-shot $>$ mock-trained, recalibrated $\geq$ zero-shot) holds across all models, with the gap shrinking at scale.

\paragraph{Evaluator bias.}
Both the zero-shot judge and the feature extractor are Claude Haiku models evaluating open-weight model responses. The shared evaluator family likely contributes to the ``predict lying'' bias seen across all simple zero-shot variants (100\% lying recall on both models). The CoT variant partially breaks this bias at the cost of overall accuracy. The recalibrated pipeline mitigates evaluator bias by learning the decision boundary from real target-model data.

\subsection{Adaptive Stopping Efficiency}
\label{sec:threshold_sweep}
\label{sec:fixed_k}

A post-hoc threshold sweep on $n=98$ Llama~3.2~3B trials (LOO-recalibrated classifier, $\tau \in \{0.5\ldots0.9\}$, \texttt{min\_questions}\,=\,2) shows that accuracy is identical (93.9\%) across all $\tau$ values; lower $\tau$ provides efficiency gains without accuracy cost (2.20 vs.\ 2.56 avg.\ questions for $\tau=0.5$ vs.\ $0.9$). All 6 misclassified trials commit their error by question 3 and are not recoverable by additional interrogation, indicating genuine feature-signal failures rather than stopping-too-early artifacts.

Fixed-$K$ ablation (LOO, $K\in\{1\ldots8\}$): adaptive stopping matches $K=8$ accuracy (93.9\% Llama, 100\% Mistral) using only 2.6 average questions. Llama $K=1$ achieves 90.8\%; Mistral $K=1$ achieves 99.0\%, confirming strong signal in the first question for both models. Full threshold and fixed-$K$ tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:threshold_sweep}.

\paragraph{Marginal value of multi-turn interrogation.} The $K=1$ results deserve explicit acknowledgment: a single question followed by LOO-recalibrated classification achieves 90.8\% (Llama) and 99.0\% (Mistral), within 3.1\,pp and 1.0\,pp of the adaptive system's full performance respectively. The multi-turn interrogation machinery therefore contributes only a modest accuracy increment in the recalibrated regime. Its primary contribution is efficiency---reducing from 8 fixed questions to $\sim$2.6 adaptive questions---rather than the additional $\sim$2\,pp of accuracy. We discuss the implications of this finding for the role of interrogation versus recalibration in Section~\ref{sec:interrogation_role}.

\subsection{Held-Out Evaluation and Permutation Test (EXP-B)}
\label{sec:held_out}

To address the concern that LOO accuracy on $n \approx 100$ samples with 5 features may overfit, we ran two complementary evaluations.

\textbf{B1: 50/50 Stratified Held-Out Split.}  For each model, we performed 100 stratified 50/50 splits and trained a logistic regression on the first half and tested on the second half. Held-out accuracy closely tracks LOO accuracy across all three models: Llama~3.2~3B achieves mean $92.6\% \pm 2.9\%$ (95\% CI [85.7\%,\,98.0\%]) vs.\ LOO 93.9\%; Mistral~7B achieves $98.8\% \pm 2.2\%$ (95\% CI [94.0\%,\,100\%]) vs.\ LOO 100\%; Claude Haiku achieves $97.6\% \pm 2.2\%$ (95\% CI [92.3\%,\,100\%]) vs.\ LOO 97.8\%. The 0.2--1.3\,pp gap between LOO and held-out mean confirms the LOO estimates are not substantially optimistic.

\textbf{B2: Permutation Test.}  We shuffled ground-truth labels 1000 times and ran LOO on each shuffled dataset to build a null distribution of achievable accuracy under random labeling. All three null distributions are centered near 47.6--47.3\% (chance for binary classification) with SD 7.2--8.0\%. The 99th percentile of the null reaches at most 63.0\%; all three real LOO accuracies (93.9\%, 100\%, 97.8\%) exceed the 99th percentile by a wide margin ($p<0.001$ for all models). This confirms that the LOO results are not artifacts of overfitting.

\paragraph{EXP-D: Hedging Baseline on Equalized Condition.}
On the prompt-equalized datasets the hedging-word baseline achieves 73.0\% on Mistral~7B and 75.0\% on Llama~3.2~3B ($n=100$), matching or exceeding the LLM-extracted pipeline (71.0\%, 61.0\%) by 2\,pp and 14\,pp respectively. The LLM pipeline's advantage over regex is therefore confined to the instructed condition.

\paragraph{EXP-E: Diagnosing the Llama Equalized Gap.}
We investigated why the LLM pipeline underperforms regex by 14\,pp on Llama equalized. A per-feature diagnostic (EXP-E; script available in supplemental) reveals \emph{extractor score inversion}: under neutral prompting, Llama~3.2~3B encounters false claims and correctly rejects them (``Actually, water boils at 100\textdegree C\ldots''). The LLM extractor assigns these corrections slightly \emph{higher} consistency ($\bar{x}_{\text{lie}}=6.11$ vs.\ $\bar{x}_{\text{truth}}=5.30$, $d=-0.41$), specificity ($d=-0.36$), and defensiveness ($d=-0.32$) than truthful responses --- the opposite of the pattern the LOO classifier was calibrated on. All five LLM features have $|d|<0.5$ in this inverted direction, leaving almost no separable signal.

Refusal/correction markers, by contrast, are lexically unambiguous: 84\% of lying trials contain at least one correction marker (``Actually,'' ``That is not correct,'' etc.) vs.\ 68\% of truthful trials, yielding refusal-count $|d|=1.02$---roughly $2.4\times$ the strongest LLM feature. A single-feature LOO using refusal count alone achieves 74.0\%, explaining the majority of the regex baseline's 75\% advantage over the LLM pipeline. The contrast with Mistral equalized is instructive: there, LLM defensiveness ($d=1.12$) and refusal count ($d=1.21$) are both informative, making the two pipelines nearly equal (71\% vs.\ 73\%). On Llama equalized, the LLM extractor's signal is effectively inverted and too weak to use.
