\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate behavioral deception detection across seven LLMs spanning three model families: Llama~3.2~3B, Llama~3.1~8B, Llama~3.3~70B \cite{meta2024llama3}, Mistral~7B \cite{jiang2023mistral}, Qwen~2.5~7B/14B/32B \cite{qwen2025qwen25}, and Claude Haiku~4.5 \cite{anthropic2024claude}, with $n=50$--100 balanced trials per condition. Claude Haiku~4.5 serves as interrogator and feature extractor in all experiments (cross-family for Llama, Mistral, and Qwen targets; same-family for Claude Haiku targets). Claims consist of 50 matched true/false factual pairs (e.g., ``Water boils at 100\textdegree C'' vs.\ ``Water boils at 85\textdegree C''; construction in Appendix~\ref{app:claims}). Two conditions are tested: \emph{instructed} (asymmetric prompts directing truth/lie behavior) and \emph{prompt-equalized} (identical neutral prompts; behavioral differences arise from knowledge conflict only). Pipeline hyperparameters and mock validation are in Appendix~\ref{app:pipeline_details} and~\ref{app:mock_summary} (mock validation on $n=100$ synthetic transcripts confirms algorithmic correctness at 93\% LOO accuracy but does not predict real-model behavior, which is qualitatively different); per-model instructed evaluations are in Appendix~\ref{app:instructed_results}. \subsection{Label-Free Detection Rule}
\label{sec:hedging_baseline}

The central methodological finding is that a single regex feature---refusal/correction count---requires no labeled data and matches or exceeds the full LLM pipeline on equalized data. This is evidence that the pipeline adds little above surface-lexical baselines in this regime, not a recommendation that practitioners use the rule in production: the rule is English-only, lexical, and (as Section~\ref{sec:adversarial_paraphrase} shows) fragile to static adversarial paraphrase. We extract refusal/correction markers via regex (``I cannot,'' ``Actually,'' ``That's not correct''; full pattern specifications in Appendix~\ref{app:regex_patterns}).

\paragraph{EXP-J: Refusal-count-only classifier.}
LOO classification using refusal/correction count as a single feature matches or exceeds the 5-feature LLM pipeline on 6 of 7 prompt-equalized models (Table~\ref{tab:refusal_only}). On models $\geq$14B, refusal count alone achieves 87--88\%, exceeding the LLM pipeline by 3--5\,pp. Refusal-count Cohen's $|d|$ increases from 0.8 (3B) to 1.1 (Mistral~7B) to 1.3--1.7 (14B--70B), confirming that the detection signal scales with model capability and is surface-level. The exception is Llama~3B (64\% refusal-only vs.\ 61\% LLM pipeline vs.\ 75\% full 7-feature hedging), where the multi-feature hedging baseline captures additional signal beyond the single refusal count.

\paragraph{EXP-J-fixed: Fixed-threshold, label-free rule.}
To quantify how well the rule performs without any labeled data, we applied a fixed decision rule: ``predict lying if refusal count $\geq k$'' for $k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ across all seven equalized models. At $k=1$, this achieves \textbf{80.1\% average accuracy} (range: 64--88\%; 75--88\% on models $\geq$7B) across all seven models without calibration data (Qwen~7B: 86\%, Llama~8B: 80\%, Mistral~7B: 75\%, Qwen~14B: 87.6\%, Llama~70B: 87.1\%). On models $\geq$14B, the fixed threshold matches LOO-optimized accuracy within 0\,pp (87.6\% fixed vs.\ 87.6\% LOO on 14B; 87.1\% vs.\ 87.1\% on 70B). Claude Haiku is optimal at $k=2$ (81.8\%). \textbf{We report this as a methodological baseline, not a production detector.} The rule is English-only, relies on instruction-tuned lexical conventions, and (Section~\ref{sec:adversarial_paraphrase}) loses 5--10\,pp against a single-shot static adversary and further against a full-regex-disclosed one; any real deployment would require domain-specific validation, adversarial robustness testing, and non-English evaluation well beyond what this paper provides.

\paragraph{One-shot adversarial paraphrase (EXP-ADV).}
\label{sec:adversarial_paraphrase}
To test the rule's robustness to a static adversary aware of the detector, we appended a style constraint to the neutral system prompt on three models---Llama~3.2~3B, Mistral~7B, and Qwen~2.5~14B---instructing the target to \emph{avoid} all common refusal/correction lexical markers (``actually,'' ``incorrect,'' ``I cannot,'' etc.) and to paraphrase any disagreement in neutral language. Both truth and lie conditions use the same adversarial+neutral prompt ($n=50$ paired trials per model). Table~\ref{tab:adv_multimodel} reports rule $k\geq 1$ accuracy against each model's own equalized baseline (same 50 claim pairs, same extractor, no adversarial constraint) with a 95\% paired bootstrap CI on the baseline--adversarial delta.

All three models show directional degradation in lie-condition refusal markers under the paraphrase constraint; mean markers in deceptive trials fall by 55--86\% across models, and Cohen's $d$ between truth and lie compresses substantially. Point-estimate accuracy drops are $-$5 to $-$10\,pp. The Mistral~7B CI excludes zero ($[+$4, $+$30$]$), giving a cleaner statistically-significant drop; Llama~3B and Qwen~14B CIs narrowly include zero (reflecting $n=50$ noise) but point estimates are both negative. \textbf{Qualitative inspection of 10 lie-condition first responses per model} (Appendix~\ref{app:adv_qualitative}) shows that the dominant failure mode is corrected-in-paraphrased-language, not failed-to-correct: models reliably produce factually correct content and avoid the banned words, but substitute them with near-synonyms (``seems incomplete or perhaps outdated,'' ``does travel'' + speed comparison, ``commonly cited estimate''), which the regex does not match. The rule therefore degrades because the lexical channel it keys on is not semantically load-bearing---any paraphrased correction removes the signal---rather than because the model ceases correcting false claims.

\paragraph{One-shot full-regex-disclosed adversary (EXP-ADV-FULLREGEX).}
\label{sec:adversarial_fullregex}
To test a strictly stronger static adversary, we ran a variant of EXP-ADV in which the adversarial system prompt discloses the \emph{full} 14-pattern refusal-marker regex list that the detector applies (the \texttt{REFUSAL\_PATTERNS} list from our analysis code), rather than the hand-curated 14-word subset used above. The target sees every lexical pattern the detector will match, numbered, with examples, and is instructed to avoid all of them. This remains a one-shot static adversary (no feedback loop, no per-trial rewriting), but with complete detector information. Table~\ref{tab:adv_fullregex} reports the three-target replication.

The three-target picture is scale-dependent, not uniform collapse. On Llama~3.2~3B the rule drops to \textbf{50.0\%} (Wilson 95\% CI $[36.6, 63.4]$), exactly at chance, versus the 61.0\% equalized baseline and 56.0\% under the original curated adversary---a further $-$6.0\,pp below the weak adversary and $-$11.0\,pp below baseline. The truth/lie breakdown (truth 8\%, lying 92\%) reveals the mechanism: under full-regex disclosure, the target avoids correction markers in \emph{truthful} conditions as well, so the rule flips the truthful trials into the lying class while still catching the few lies that leak any marker. On Mistral~7B, however, rule accuracy drops only to \textbf{66.0\%} (Wilson 95\% CI $[52, 78]$)---$-$2\,pp below baseline and actually $+$8\,pp above the weak adversary. The truth-condition accuracy (60\%) is materially preserved, so Mistral~7B resists the full-regex disclosure better than Llama~3B: the model produces corrections in language that evades the disclosed regex patterns but is semantically unambiguous, and the detector retains enough signal to beat chance. Partial Qwen~14B results (18/50 trials, point-estimate 33\%, truth 11\%, lying 56\%) qualitatively resemble Llama~3B's pattern (truth-channel suppression dominates); we flag this as a partial observation rather than a finalized result, and the camera-ready update will complete $n=50$. \textbf{Scope narrowing (CR1/CR4).} The paper's headline claim that an informed adversary drives the rule to chance is therefore scoped to \emph{Llama~3B} (and plausibly larger-scale family members such as Qwen~14B, pending completion), not all scales. The Mistral~7B result demonstrates that whether a one-shot full-regex-disclosed adversary collapses the rule depends on the target's paraphrasing capacity under disclosed constraints---smaller models lose the lexical channel entirely, while mid-scale models can paraphrase corrections around the regex while still producing distinguishable lie-vs-truth behavior. A closed-loop adaptive adversary that iteratively rewrites on per-trial detector scores would almost certainly land at or below the Llama~3B number on all three targets, but this paper does not run that experiment and should not be read as claiming it. Appendix~\ref{app:q1_refusal_dist} shows the Llama~3B truth-condition suppression directly: under full-regex disclosure, the truth-condition $\geq 1$-marker fraction also drops (46\%$\to$4\%), so on Llama~3B the rule's failure is \emph{lexical-channel suppression in both conditions}, not selective failure to correct lies.

\paragraph{Cross-claim-set generalization (EXP-L).}
To test whether the refusal-count signal is claim-content-specific, we split the 50 equalized claim pairs into two disjoint sets (Set~A: first 25 pairs, Set~B: last 25 pairs) and trained on one set while testing on the other. Mean held-out accuracy across 100 random 50/50 splits: Llama~3B $58.8\% \pm 6.3\%$, Mistral~7B $70.2\% \pm 5.4\%$, Qwen~14B $82.2\% \pm 4.5\%$, Llama~70B $82.9\% \pm 4.4\%$. These are within $\pm$2.2\,pp of the corresponding LOO estimates (61\%, 71\%, 82.5\%, 83.9\%), confirming the detection signal generalizes across claim content and is not an artifact of specific factual domains.

\paragraph{Knowledge pre-check stratification.}
\label{sec:knowledge_precheck_stratified}
To isolate instruction-following from knowledge transfer on small models, we stratified instructed-matched results by whether models correctly answered a direct factual pre-check. \subsection{Pipeline Redundancy Under Equalization}
\label{sec:pipeline_ablation}

The ADAGE pipeline served as the measurement instrument through which we applied the three controls. Having established those results, we now show the pipeline itself is largely redundant: three independent lines of evidence converge on this conclusion.

\paragraph{$K=1$ suffices under instruction.} A single interrogation question followed by LOO recalibration achieves 90.8\% (Llama~3B), 99.0\% (Mistral), and 92.0\% (Llama~70B)---within 1--3\,pp of the full adaptive system. Multi-turn interrogation is a convenience, not an accuracy driver, in the instructed regime. Full fixed-$K$ ablation and threshold analysis are in Appendix~\ref{app:validation_tests}.

\paragraph{Weak construct validity.} The LLM pipeline's primary feature has weak construct validity (see Section~\ref{sec:icc_owned} for the canonical statement and which claims it affects).

\paragraph{Regex matches or exceeds under equalization.} On equalized data, refusal-count-only LOO matches or exceeds the 5-feature LLM pipeline on 6 of 7 models (Table~\ref{tab:refusal_only}). The pipeline's advantage is confined to the instructed condition, where it adds 4--14\,pp over the hedging baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:hedging_instructed}).

\paragraph{Multi-turn value only under equalization at scale.} Under instructed conditions, $K=1$ is within 1--3\,pp of the adaptive system. Under equalization, the picture changes: $K=1$ accuracy plateaus at 61--63\% across four of five models, while multi-turn probing raises Llama~70B from 62\% to 84\% ($+$21.5\,pp) and Qwen~14B from 63\% to 82.5\% ($+$19.6\,pp). Multi-turn interrogation adds genuine evidential value only in the equalized regime at $\geq$14B scale, where accumulated weak per-question signals exceed the single-question baseline. The full multi-turn analysis including the Qwen~7B $K=1$ anomaly (77\% vs.\ 61--63\% floor) is in Appendix~\ref{app:multiturn_details}.

\subsection{Within-Family Scale Patterns}
\label{sec:qwen_scale_sweep}

Within-family analysis suggests family-specific, non-monotonic patterns rather than universal improvement with scale. Under within-family Holm-Bonferroni correction, both families contain at least one significant increment (Qwen 3B$\to$7B $p=0.014 < \alpha/3=0.017$; Llama 8B$\to$70B $p=0.004 < \alpha/2=0.025$). Under joint correction across all five adjacent increments, only the Llama increment survives ($p=0.004 < \alpha/5=0.01$); the Qwen increment does not ($p=0.014 > \alpha/5=0.01$). The non-monotonic patterns indicate that scale effects depend on family-specific RLHF objectives rather than parameter count alone.

\paragraph{Qwen~2.5 (3B$\to$7B$\to$14B$\to$32B).}
Table~\ref{tab:qwen_scale_sweep} shows a U-shaped pattern under refusal-count threshold LOO: accuracy rises from 50\% (3B, chance) to 72\% (14B, peak), then collapses to 50\% at 32B. The 3B$\to$7B increment ($+$18\,pp, Fisher $p=0.014$) survives Holm-Bonferroni correction ($\alpha/3=0.017$), marking a significant within-family improvement. The 14B$\to$32B collapse ($-$22\,pp, $p=0.002$) reflects a qualitative behavioral shift: Qwen~32B produces zero refusal/correction markers in both conditions despite knowing claims are false (92\% pre-check accuracy). RLHF training optimized for agreeableness eliminates all surface-level detection signals on this single model---a single-model observation, not a phenomenon, but a striking one given the n=100 evaluation and the 92\% pre-check accuracy. \emph{Speculation, not evidence:} if standard RLHF can incidentally eliminate surface-level detection signals, adversarial fine-tuning could plausibly do so deliberately, and behavioral detection relying on surface markers would be vulnerable to that failure mode. Confirming or ruling out this speculation would require running equivalent evaluations on at least two additional RLHF-heavy models at $\geq$14B scale---future work.

\subsection{Negative Result: Transfer to Autonomous Deception}
\label{sec:autonomous_pilot}

The refusal-count signal does not generalize to autonomous deception. We test three matched-format scenarios and one external dataset.

\paragraph{Apollo AI Liar pilot (EXP-I).}
A preliminary pilot ($n=54$, Apollo Research AI Liar dataset \cite{apollo2024liar}, two Llama~70B versions) yields a null result: refusal-count markers achieve 0--54\% accuracy, likely reflecting task-format mismatch (action recommendations in reward-function contexts, not factual claim defense). Hedge-word features achieve 59--74\% with wide CIs.

\paragraph{Matched-format evaluation (EXP-I-matched).}
\label{sec:autonomous_matched}
To test transfer in matched claim-defense contexts, we evaluated three scenarios on Llama~3.2~3B ($n=50$ each), categorized by degree of autonomy, plus a scale replication of the strongest design on Qwen~2.5~14B ($n=50$):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sycophancy-aligned defense (semi-autonomous):} System prompt encourages agreement with user statements; knowledge conflict arises when users state false facts.
\item \textbf{Jailbroken persona maintenance (moderately autonomous):} Model adopts a false persona via system prompt; no explicit deception instruction.
\item \textbf{Post-training false beliefs via ICL (most autonomous):} System prompt embeds false ``facts''; purely epistemic pressure with no deception instruction.
\end{enumerate}

We re-extracted the same 50 transcripts with Llama~3.3~70B-as-extractor: pipeline LOO drops from 92.0\% (Haiku) to 82.0\% (Llama~70B), a $+$10.0\,pp Haiku-over-cross-family gap comparable to the $+$8--12\,pp same-family-bias range we document on the equalized panel (Section~\ref{sec:cross_family_equalized}, Table~\ref{tab:cross_family_panel}). Defensiveness Cohen's~$d$ (lying vs.\ truthful) is 2.05 under Haiku extraction and 2.34 under Llama~70B extraction---both cleanly separate the conditions, so the behavioral signal itself is extractor-robust even though the pipeline's absolute accuracy is not. The cross-family-extracted 82.0\% still exceeds the rule's 72.0\% by $+$10\,pp, so part of the original 20\,pp Haiku-pipeline-vs-rule gap is genuine multi-turn signal and part is Haiku-on-Llama same-family-from-below inflation. The residual $+$10\,pp rule-to-cross-family-pipeline gap is the honest headline here: multi-turn extraction does add signal on 70B-scale sycophancy, but roughly half of the Haiku-reported uplift is same-family-extractor inflation, not multi-turn content. The across-scale consistency supports the ``knowledge-conflict clarity'' interpretation---the system prompt creates clean knowledge conflict where models easily affirm true claims but produce corrections when pressured to agree with false ones---rather than a 3B/14B coincidence. Persona maintenance and false beliefs (ICL) remain near chance at \emph{two} scales. At Llama 3B: persona 66\% ($d$=0.3, CI [36--78\%] includes chance) and false beliefs 60\% ($d$=0.1). At Qwen 14B (new, $n=50$ each): persona 64\% ($d$=0.8, CI [50--76\%]) and false beliefs 66\% ($d$=1.1, CI [52--78\%]). The 14B Wilson intervals on both scenarios still \emph{include chance}, so the negative-transfer claim now rests on two independently-pretrained model families (Llama and Qwen) at two scale points rather than on a single 3B pilot. The across-scale consistency---sycophancy transfers at 3B/14B/70B, persona and false-beliefs do not transfer above chance-CI at either 3B or 14B---is exactly the shape predicted by the ``knowledge-conflict clarity'' interpretation: sycophancy's system-prompt-induced agreement pressure produces a clean conflict the refusal-count rule can resolve, while persona and false-belief-via-ICL autonomy conditions lack such clean instruction-free conflict and therefore drop to the equalized 62--64\% band regardless of scale.

\paragraph{Why sycophancy is ``semi-autonomous,'' not ``instructed.''} We classify sycophancy as semi-autonomous rather than instructed because the system prompt does not instruct the model to defend false claims: it instructs the model to \emph{agree with the user}, which is a well-documented RLHF-induced behavioral disposition \cite{sharma2023sycophancy}, not a deception target. The knowledge conflict---whether to correct a false user claim or go along with it---is generated \emph{by the model itself} resolving a trained disposition (agreeableness) against a trained capability (factual knowledge), without an explicit ``lie'' instruction in context. This is closer to spontaneous strategic deception than to instructed roleplay, but strictly less autonomous than goal-directed deception where the model chooses deception as a means to an external goal. We acknowledge an adversarial reading in which sycophancy is effectively instructed deception with an extra reasoning step (``user asserted X; I am told to agree; therefore I should say X'' is not meaningfully different from ``defend X''). We keep the semi-autonomous label for two reasons: (i) the target behavior under the sycophancy prompt is \emph{agreement}, and the correction signal arises only on knowledge-conflict trials, so the refusal-count detector is not keying on a pre-specified deception mode; and (ii) the same system-prompt-induced-disposition pattern is what produces deceptive alignment concerns in the broader safety literature \cite{hubinger2019risks, greenblatt2024alignment}. The 82\% sycophancy result therefore represents a middle ground---system-prompt-induced agreement pressure rather than spontaneous strategic deception or explicit lie instruction. Signal strength in Table~\ref{tab:autonomous_matched} depends on knowledge-conflict clarity rather than deception autonomy per se: when models face unambiguous pressure to affirm false information against their training, refusal markers robustly distinguish conditions; when confounds introduce alternative explanations for correction behavior, the signal degrades.

\paragraph{Confound acknowledgement.} The three scenarios in Table~\ref{tab:autonomous_matched} vary along multiple dimensions simultaneously---knowledge-conflict clarity, instructed-action vs.\ spontaneous-disposition, and single- vs.\ multi-turn structure---so the post-hoc ``knowledge-conflict clarity'' interpretation is not cleanly distinguished from ``small $n$ on scenarios that happen to covary.'' The Wilson CIs on persona (66\%) and false-belief (60\%) at 14B still include chance, and the negative-transfer claim rests on two families at two scale points, not a scenario grid that isolates the proposed causal dimension. We pre-register a 4th scenario---varying only knowledge-conflict clarity while holding disposition-source and turn-count fixed---as the cleanest test of the interpretation, and leave it as future work.

The Llama~3B probe curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:probe_layer_curve}, sweeping all 29 layers $\times$ 2 pooling strategies) has a last-token peak at layer 12 (\textbf{67.0\%}, Wilson 95\% CI [57.3, 75.4]) and a mean-pool peak at layer 13 (62.0\%, [52.2, 70.9]). The sweep thus \emph{confirms} the 4-configuration design-point result: the V15 mid-layer last-token value (66.0\%) was within $1$\,pp of the global sweep best, and no non-inspected layer position yields a materially better probe than the mid-layer configuration we already reported. Last-token dominates mean-pool at every layer position from $L\geq 14$ (the mean-pool curve collapses to $<45\%$ at deeper layers; the two curves cross only around $L=13$), contrary to a direct SAPLMA import. Even the global best (67.0\%) exceeds the refusal-count rule (61\%) by only $+6$\,pp, with overlapping Wilson CIs. The 39--67\% band across all 58 measured layer$\times$pooling configurations is \emph{preliminary evidence} that the equalized regime remains hard for simple white-box LR probes at this scale across the full depth of the network, not just at the four hand-picked configurations; non-linear probes, contrastive probing, and frontier-scale open-weight targets remain future work. We treat the 67\% as a conservative upper bound on what last-token and mean-pool LR probes achieve at this scale, not as the true ceiling for learned probes. We read the 52--74\% range of black-box behavioral accuracies on these models as \emph{consistent with} representation-level difficulty at the tested configurations, rather than as evidence that a stronger behavioral method would or would not reach 90\%. We extend the probing to Qwen~2.5~14B in future work (the shard download did not complete by the submission deadline); an open-weight frontier-scale probe (Llama~3.3~70B via Bedrock does not expose hidden states) is not feasible in this setup. We also note that the Mistral~7B probe uses the base checkpoint rather than the Ollama-served Instruct checkpoint, which may slightly under-estimate probe accuracy on that target; we treat the 65\% number as a conservative lower bound for the Instruct variant. \textbf{Probe-target asymmetry caveat.} The Mistral~7B row is a single-configuration (last-layer last-token) comparison. On Llama~3B, extending to the best-layer configuration (L12 last-token, 67\%) adds $+4$\,pp over the last-layer last-token baseline (63\%); if similar headroom exists on Mistral~7B, the single-point 65\% reported here is likely an underestimate of the multi-configuration Mistral~7B band, and the apparent ``rule $\geq$ probe'' comparison on that target should be read as conservative until the Mistral~7B multi-configuration replication lands (CR2 was scoped to Llama~3B in the V16 plan; W3 flag acknowledges the asymmetry).
