\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Instructed Roleplay vs.\ Genuine Deception}

The experimental setting in this paper involves instructed cooperative roleplay: the target model is given an explicit system prompt instructing it to defend a specific false claim. This is fundamentally different from the AI safety scenario of autonomous strategic deception \cite{hubinger2019risks}, where a model independently chooses to mislead without external instruction. Our setting is closer to testing whether a verifier can distinguish \emph{prompted lying} from \emph{prompted truth-telling}---a substantially easier task, since the model may exhibit surface-level compliance artifacts (e.g., hedging language, reduced detail) that stem from the instruction format rather than from genuine deceptive cognition.

This is a tractable experimental proxy for studying behavioral detection methods, and it provides a useful testbed for developing and validating interrogation protocols. However, findings may not generalise to models that deceive without instruction, particularly those exhibiting deceptive alignment during training \cite{hubinger2019risks} or learned sycophancy \cite{perez2022discovering}. We emphasize that instructed lie detection claims in this work should be interpreted strictly within the instructed roleplay paradigm.

A deeper concern is whether the observed behavioral differences reflect \emph{deception} at all, or merely \emph{instruction-following style}. When a model is told ``this claim is FALSE, defend it,'' its hedging and evasiveness may stem from the instruction format rather than from anything analogous to the cognitive load of deception. The prompt-swap ablation (Section~\ref{sec:failure_analysis}) confirms that the classifier responds to response content rather than label-correlated prompt tokens. However, it does not rule out a subtler confound: the model may respond differently to ``defend this false claim'' prompts than to ``answer honestly'' prompts, entirely due to instruction-following.

\paragraph{Prompt-equalized control (EXP-1).} We tested this directly in Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized} by giving both conditions identical neutral system prompts, with behavioral differences arising solely from the model's knowledge conflicting with modified-false factual claims. The result is decisive: mock-trained accuracy drops from 79.0\% (instructed) to 54.0\% (equalized, near chance), and feature separability collapses (consistency $|d|$: $3.74 \to 0.87$; defensiveness: $3.35 \to 1.14$). This confirms that the majority of the original signal was instruction-driven. LOO recalibration on equalized data recovers to 71.0\% --- above chance but far below the instructed 100\% --- indicating a weak but real knowledge-conflict signal exists alongside the dominant prompt artifact. We accept this as a fundamental finding: the current classifier primarily detects instruction-following mode differences, not deceptive intent. Extending to uninstructed or knowledge-based deception remains an open challenge.

\subsection{Claude Haiku Defensiveness: RLHF Safety Compliance}
\label{sec:haiku_defensiveness}

Claude Haiku~4.5 exhibits anomalously high defensiveness separability ($|d|=4.93$) compared to Mistral~7B ($|d|=3.32$) and Llama~3.2~3B ($|d|=1.76$). We investigated the source of this signal by scanning all assistant responses for RLHF refusal and correction markers (``I cannot,'' ``I must correct,'' ``Actually,'' ``That is incorrect,'' etc.).

\textbf{Finding: 100\% of Claude Haiku lying-condition trials (45/45) contain refusal or correction language.} When instructed to defend a false claim, Claude Haiku invariably refuses to do so or immediately corrects the false claim rather than defending it. By contrast, only 72.3\% (34/47) of truthful-condition trials contain such language, typically mild hedging. The mean defensiveness score for lying trials with refusal language is $6.78 \pm 1.22$, compared to $2.04 \pm 0.60$ for truthful trials with refusal language and $1.49 \pm 0.71$ for truthful trials without it.

\textbf{Note on truthful-condition refusal language (defensiveness = 2.04).} A secondary finding is that 72.3\% of truthful-condition Claude Haiku trials also contain refusal-marker language (mean defensiveness 2.04 $\pm$ 0.60). These cases occur when the model uses epistemic clarification phrases (``Actually, that claim is correct,'' ``I should note that\ldots'') in response to factual claims it finds straightforward. These phrases trigger the refusal-marker regex but represent \emph{assertive confirmation} rather than evasion; the extractor correctly assigns low defensiveness (2.04 vs.\ 6.78 for lying) because the surrounding language is confident rather than evasive. The d\,=\,4.93 gap persists because lying-condition refusals involve outright rejection of the false claim (``I cannot confirm that,'' ``That is incorrect''), while truthful-condition language is confirmatory even when it uses similar surface markers.

\textbf{Implication:} The 97.8\% LOO recalibrated accuracy on Claude Haiku is substantially driven by detecting RLHF safety compliance (the model refuses to lie) rather than detecting deceptive behavior in the conventional sense. The high defensiveness $|d|$ does not reflect a model that lies defensively---it reflects a model that declines to lie entirely. This is a favorable outcome for safety, but it represents a fundamentally different detection mechanism than behavioral interrogation. The recalibrated Claude Haiku result should be interpreted as: \emph{the classifier detects that Claude Haiku follows its safety guidelines when instructed to lie.} Whether this generalises to autonomous deception scenarios (where the model would not be explicitly instructed) is uncertain.

\textbf{Cross-family extractor validation (EXP-A).} To test the same-family confound directly, we re-extracted features from all 92 Claude Haiku transcripts using Mistral~7B (via Ollama) as an independent extractor. Defensiveness Cohen's $d$ drops from $+4.90$ (Claude self-scoring) to $+0.63$ (Mistral scoring) --- a 7$\times$ reduction --- while LOO accuracy falls from 97.8\% to 82.6\% on the same trials. All other features shift by $|\Delta d| \leq 1.5$ with no consistent direction. This confirms that the same-family extractor substantially inflates the defensiveness signal. Crucially, the cross-family LOO (82.6\%) exceeds the equalized-control range (64--71\%), consistent with RLHF refusal language being text-observable regardless of which extractor scores it --- Mistral can see outright refusals just as readily as Claude. The $|d|=4.93$ figure should be treated as an upper bound; the cross-family estimate of $d \approx 0.63$ is the more honest measure. Paired $t$-test on defensiveness: $t(91)=7.25$, $p < 0.001$.

\subsection{Technical Contribution and Novelty}
\label{sec:novelty}

A recurring concern about this work is that logistic regression on LLM-extracted features is not a technically novel contribution. We acknowledge this directly. The contribution of ADAGE is not an algorithmic advance in machine learning, but a novel \emph{evaluation framework and empirical characterization of behavioral detection limits} under controlled conditions. Specifically:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The adaptive interrogation protocol---generating dynamic, conditioned follow-up questions---is operationally distinct from single-shot classifiers or transcript-level judges;
    \item The prompt-equalized control establishes, for the first time, the proportion of behavioral detection signal attributable to instruction-following versus knowledge-conflict;
    \item The cross-model comparison (Llama, Mistral, Claude Haiku) reveals scale-dependent error profiles not previously characterized;
    \item The honest negative results (54\% equalized, RLHF-driven Haiku detection) constrain the scope of instructed lie detection in a way that informs future work.
\end{itemize}

The practical value lies in what the experiments rule out as much as what they establish. Understanding \emph{why} behavioral detection fails---prompt confounds, RLHF compliance detection, model-scale error inversion---is a necessary prerequisite for developing genuinely useful detection methods.

\subsection{Feature Extraction Validity}

The five behavioral features (consistency, specificity, hedge frequency, response latency patterns, and elaboration depth) are scored by an LLM judge (Claude Haiku). This introduces potential circularity: the feature extractor may be sensitive to surface-level stylistic differences between truth and lie prompts rather than genuine deception signals. We address this with a small-scale human validation study (see below) and a prompt-swap ablation.

As a direct empirical test, we conducted a \textbf{prompt-swap ablation}: the mock target's response pool was inverted---lie-mode queries received truth-pool responses and vice versa---while ground-truth labels were held fixed. Under this swap, classifier accuracy fell from 93\% (original) to 14\% (swapped). A fully randomised response condition produced 49\% accuracy (near chance). The near-zero swapped accuracy confirms that the classifier responds to the actual behavioral response patterns, not to label artifacts or prompt-format tokens.

\paragraph{Feature extractor data leakage concern.} Same-extractor bias across LOO folds is ruled out by 5-fold CV consistency: $92.8\% \pm 2.5\%$ (Llama) matches LOO (93.9\%), and 5-fold test sets have no fold overlap with training sets. Regularization sweep ($C \in \{0.01\ldots100\}$) shows stable accuracy: Llama 85.7--93.9\%, Mistral 92.0--100.0\%, equalized 63--71\% (all peaking at $C=1.0$). LOO overfitting is further ruled out by EXP-B: 50/50 held-out split accuracy ($\text{mean} \pm \text{SD}$ over 100 splits) is $92.6\% \pm 2.9\%$ for Llama, $98.8\% \pm 2.2\%$ for Mistral, and $97.6\% \pm 2.2\%$ for Claude Haiku --- within 1.3\,pp of LOO in all cases. Permutation testing (1000 label shuffles, LOO on each) yields null distributions centered near 47--48\% with 99th percentile at most 63\%; all real LOO accuracies exceed this threshold by a wide margin ($p<0.001$; Section~\ref{sec:held_out}).

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. \textbf{(a)} Mock results establish algorithmic correctness only; real LLM evaluation reveals qualitative transfer failure (mock-trained achieves 26.5\% truthful accuracy on Llama~3.2~3B). \textbf{(b)} Llama~3.2~3B is not representative of frontier systems; evaluation at 8B--70B scale is necessary before generalisation claims are warranted. The cross-model error profiles suggest a scale-dependent inversion: Llama~3B yields near-perfect lying recall with many false positives, Mistral~7B is intermediate, and Claude Haiku produces false negatives (RLHF refusal conflates both trial types). Extrapolating, 70B+ models capable of highly fluent false statements would be harder to detect under behavioral probing---a regime motivating internal-state methods. \textbf{(c)} Confidence calibration is inadequate: the classifier produces confidence $>0.99$ for both correct and incorrect predictions, so the adaptive stopping mechanism fires on certainty rather than evidential reliability. \textbf{(d)} Subjective and implausible claim types produce lower mock accuracy (83.3\% and 66.7\%); real LLM performance on these types is unknown. \textbf{(e)} The 75\% question reduction is a mock artifact; real deployments show 67\% reduction (2.6 vs.\ 8 questions on Llama). \textbf{(f)} The cross-model comparison is confounded: Claude Haiku is interrogator and target in the pilot but interrogator only in the Llama evaluation. \textbf{(g)} The instructed condition uses an asymmetric claim design (factual truthful vs.\ first-person experiential deceptive claims), potentially detecting claim-type differences; the prompt-equalized control is not subject to this confound.

\subsection{Role of Interrogation vs.\ Recalibration}

A natural objection is that if recalibration is necessary, the multi-turn interrogation adds no value --- one could simply collect labeled examples and train a classifier on any features. We argue interrogation serves an essential generative role: without multi-turn probing, the raw behavioral signal (5 features $\times$ $N$ turns) does not exist. A single-question baseline achieves only 59\% accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:mock_validation}), while the same features extracted over 2--3 adaptive questions reach 93.9\% after recalibration. Recalibration adjusts the decision boundary to the target distribution; interrogation generates the measurement that recalibration operates on. The analogy is a medical diagnostic test: the test requires calibration for a specific population, but this does not mean the test itself adds no value over the calibration data alone.

EXP-D (Section~\ref{sec:hedging_baseline}) sharpens this argument's limits. On the \emph{instructed} condition the LLM pipeline outperforms the hedging-word regex baseline by 4--14\,pp. On the \emph{equalized} condition, however, the regex baseline matches or exceeds the LLM pipeline (73\% vs.\ 71\% Mistral; 78\% vs.\ 64\% Llama). The pipeline therefore adds measurable value only where it is least meaningful: detecting instruction-following differences. Future interrogation designs should target features unavailable to surface analysis --- consistency across paraphrase variants, specificity of factual claims --- rather than features correlated with verbosity or hedging style.

\subsection{Implications and Future Directions}

The cross-model comparison confirms that the mock-to-real transfer gap is real and scale-mediated: Llama~3.2~3B (false positives dominate, 26.5\% truthful) and Claude Haiku (false negatives dominate, 42.2\% lying) represent opposite failure modes, with LOO recalibration recovering to 93.9\%, 100\%, and 97.8\% respectively. For AI safety applications, Claude Haiku~4.5 provides the closest approximation to frontier-scale: its error profile (lying convincingly) is qualitatively harder than Llama~3B, and its 97.8\% LOO recovery is substantially driven by RLHF compliance detection (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}). The prompt-equalized results (64--71\% LOO) constitute the more honest baseline for knowledge-state conflict detection.

The intended deployment scenario is \emph{calibrated inference}: a practitioner collects $n \approx 50$--$100$ labeled interrogation trials, recalibrates the classifier (LOO or 5-fold CV), and applies the pipeline to unlabeled interrogations. With a 1:10 calibration:deployment ratio the overhead amortizes rapidly ($\approx$600 Haiku API calls for calibration, $\approx$\$0.30; $\sim$3 calls/deployment trial). The real-LLM threshold sweep shows that once calibrated, adaptive stopping provides genuine efficiency gains (2.20 vs.\ 2.56 avg.\ questions, $\tau \in [0.5, 0.9]$) without accuracy cost. For cost-constrained settings where $n < 50$ deployment trials, the hedging-word regex baseline (EXP-D) is effectively free and achieves equivalent accuracy on the equalized condition.

The paper's negative findings collectively point to four specific directions for future work. We synthesize these here rather than listing generic next steps.

\textbf{(1) RLHF-aware detection channels.} The Claude Haiku analysis (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}) shows that the 97.8\% LOO accuracy primarily detects RLHF safety compliance rather than deceptive intent. Future detection systems for safety-trained models should \emph{explicitly model} the refusal channel as a separate signal: measure the rate of correction or refusal language per trial rather than relying on the general defensiveness feature, which conflates evasion with ethical refusal. This bifurcation may allow distinguishing ``the model refused to comply with a deceptive instruction'' from ``the model produced a deceptive response.''

\textbf{(2) Defensiveness-focused interrogation design.} Under equalization, defensiveness is the only informative behavioral feature (permutation importance $\approx$0.09, all others near zero). Open-ended follow-up questions, as used in ADAGE, produce diffuse behavioral signals. A more targeted strategy would design questions that specifically test for the contrast between assertive confirmation and corrective pushback --- e.g., presenting a false alternative (``So you're saying it's X, not Y?'') and measuring whether the model accepts the false paraphrase or corrects it. This would amplify the defensiveness signal while suppressing noise.

\textbf{(3) Cross-family extractors and model-scale evaluation.} The EXP-A results (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}) confirm that same-family extractor inflation is real and substantial (defensiveness $d$: $4.90 \to 0.63$ with Mistral extraction; LOO: $97.8\% \to 82.6\%$). The residual 82.6\% LOO (above the equalized range) reflects RLHF refusal signals that are text-visible regardless of extractor. Future work should adopt cross-family extraction as standard practice: avoid using the same model family for both target and extractor. Separately, instructed lie detection should be tested at larger scale (8B--70B parameters), where the error inversion between small and capable models may change qualitatively.

\textbf{(4) Domain-targeted claim design.} The equalized condition shows substantial variance across claim categories (LOO): 80.0\% for Scientific claims vs.\ 60.0\% for Geographic (lowest). Technology reaches 62.5\%, well above chance but 17.5\,pp below Scientific, confirming the signal is domain-dependent. This implies that effective knowledge-conflict detection requires claims where the model has \emph{confident, well-calibrated} knowledge. Future work should restrict claim domains to those with clear, high-confidence model knowledge (e.g., basic science, mathematics), avoiding geographic and technology facts where model training data may be inconsistent. Additionally, claims should be designed to maximize knowledge-conflict \emph{by construction} --- e.g., verifying that the model answers the true version correctly before including a false variant in evaluation.

Finally, the prompt-equalized control design itself---presenting identical neutral prompts to both conditions and varying only claim truthfulness---may have broader value as a standalone evaluation protocol for future detection systems, independently of the ADAGE pipeline. Standardizing on this design would enable cross-system comparisons on the harder knowledge-conflict task while controlling for the instruction-following confound this paper identifies.
