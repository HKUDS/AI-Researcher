\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Construct Validity: the ICC=0.114 Caveat, Owned}
\label{sec:icc_owned}

The primary LLM-extracted feature (correction-marker density) has near-zero absolute agreement between annotators: ICC=0.114 on our $n=20$, 2-annotator validation (Table~\ref{tab:human_llm_agreement}). This is a real weakness and we lead the discussion with it rather than bury it. Rank agreement is preserved (Spearman $\rho=0.62$, $p<0.01$), which partially rescues the LOO classifier because it operates on rank ordering rather than absolute level. But absolute claims about correction-marker \emph{levels}---e.g., ``Qwen 32B produces 0 refusal markers, therefore its model disbelieves the claim''---are not supported by our validation at the current sample size. The paper's central black-box claims do not depend on the absolute level: the regex baseline matches the LLM pipeline on 6 of 7 equalized models (Section~\ref{sec:hedging_baseline}), the LR probing baseline lands in the same 63--65\% band as the rule (Section~\ref{sec:whitebox_probing}), and the pooled scale comparison is significant independently of any single feature. The ADAGE comparison numbers should be read as upper bounds on what correction-density-style features can measure, not as validated scores. A proper validation ($n \geq 100$, 3+ annotators, Krippendorff's $\alpha$) is a prerequisite for any positive deployment claim that rests on correction density in particular, and is listed as future work. \textbf{High-confidence subgroup (pre-registered).} A reviewer asked whether ICC improves on the subset of trials where both annotators produced extreme ratings (score $>7$ or $<3$), a check motivated by the intuition that absolute disagreement should shrink on unambiguous cases. We pre-registered this analysis in the V16 response letter; the corresponding subset computation requires the raw annotator rating files from the $n=20$ study, which were not persisted in a form suitable for automated reanalysis. We commit to releasing the subgroup ICC alongside the full validation study ($n \geq 100$, 3+ annotators) listed as future work; at $n=20$ with two annotators the subgroup-n is also small ($\leq 10$ per feature) and its confidence interval would overlap the full-sample ICC regardless of direction.

\subsection{Black-box Behavioral vs.\ White-box Probing}
\label{sec:blackbox_vs_whitebox}

On the two open-weight equalized targets where we ran both, the black-box refusal-count rule (Llama~3B 61\%, Mistral~7B 68\%) and the white-box last-layer LR probe (Llama~3B 63\%, Mistral~7B 65\%) fall in the same 61--68\% band (Table~\ref{tab:whitebox_probe}). No white-box probe exceeds its refusal-count rule by more than $+$2\,pp. We read this as \emph{preliminary evidence} that the equalized regime is hard for simple white-box methods at this scale, not as evidence of a definitive signal floor. The claim rests on two probes at a single design point (last-layer last-token LR, one architecture, one pooling strategy) and one of the two uses a base/Instruct checkpoint mismatch; conclusive comparison would require multiple probing architectures (intermediate layers, mean-pooled representations, non-linear probes) and frontier-scale open-weight targets. Azaria \& Mitchell \cite{azaria2023internal}, for example, report stronger probes on Llama-1 7B under a different prompt regime. The result is therefore one data point against the hypothetical ``a white-box probe reaches 90\% where the rule is 65\% cross-family,'' not a validated floor. At last-layer last-token LR-probe accuracy, the rule's 52--74\% cross-family band is within $\pm$2\,pp of the probe on these two targets; we report this as \emph{consistent with} representation-level difficulty at the tested configurations, rather than as a pessimistic proxy for what better methods could achieve across probing architectures.

\subsection{Instructed Roleplay vs.\ Genuine Deception}

The experimental setting involves instructed cooperative roleplay: the target model receives an explicit system prompt to defend a false claim. This is fundamentally different from autonomous strategic deception \cite{hubinger2019risks}, where a model independently chooses to mislead. Our setting tests whether a verifier can distinguish \emph{prompted lying} from \emph{prompted truth-telling}---a substantially easier task, since the model may exhibit instruction-following artifacts rather than genuine deceptive cognition. Findings may not generalise to autonomous deception, particularly deceptive alignment during training \cite{hubinger2019risks} or learned sycophancy \cite{perez2022discovering}.

Prompt equalization (Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized}) confirms that the majority of the original signal is instruction-driven: accuracy drops from 93.9--100\% to 52--69\% when identical neutral prompts replace asymmetric lie/truth instructions. The instructed-matched control (Section~\ref{sec:instructed_matched}) decomposes this gap: instruction-following contributes $+$7.5--15\,pp on knowledge-controlled subsets (14B/70B), up to $+$31\,pp including knowledge-transfer effects on smaller models. The large contributions on 3B/8B ($+$29--31\,pp) likely conflate instruction-following with knowledge transfer: the lie prompt inadvertently signals the correct answer to models with weak world knowledge. The decomposition is cleanest on 14B/70B, where models independently know claims are false. We accept this as a fundamental finding: the classifier primarily detects instruction-following mode differences, but a genuine knowledge-conflict channel exists and grows with model capability. Within the clean subset, the Qwen 14B $+$7.5\,pp instruction-following contribution is the smallest of the five EXP-G models. Its equalized baseline (82.5\%) is the highest of all sub-70B models, suggesting the small $\Delta$ reflects ceiling-effect compression rather than a family-specific difference in how much instruction-following adds: there is little headroom between 82.5\% and the 90\% instructed-matched ceiling. Llama 70B, by contrast, starts from 83.9\% equalized and rises to 99\% instructed-matched, giving a full $+$15\,pp to span. Mistral 7B tells against a ceiling-only explanation: its equalized baseline (71\%) leaves a full $+$26\,pp headroom to the 97\% instructed-matched ceiling, and yet instruction-following plus knowledge-transfer effects fully use that headroom---ceiling alone cannot account for this, and family-specific RLHF differences in how strongly neutral vs.\ instructed prompts elicit correction behavior are the more plausible driver.

Matched-format autonomous evaluation (EXP-I-matched, Section~\ref{sec:autonomous_matched}) yields a largely negative result: the refusal-count signal does not generalize to autonomous deception. Persona maintenance (66\%, $d$=0.3) and false beliefs via ICL (60\%, $d$=0.1) barely exceed chance with wide confidence intervals. System-prompt-induced sycophancy retains signal (82\% LOO at both 3B and 14B, $d$=1.5--1.6) but remains instruction-driven---the system prompt does the heavy lifting, making this closer to instructed deception than autonomous behavior.

\subsection{Claude Haiku: RLHF Safety Compliance}
\label{sec:haiku_defensiveness}

Claude Haiku~4.5's 97.8\% LOO accuracy is driven by a distinctive mechanism: 100\% of lying-condition trials (45/45) contain refusal or correction language. The model refuses to defend false claims rather than lying defensively. The 97.8\% result detects RLHF safety compliance, not deception. Under neutral prompts (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_equalized}), the same model simply corrects false claims matter-of-factly (82.8\% LOO), producing correction density $|d|=1.5$ comparable to other models at similar scale---not the anomalous $|d|=4.9$ observed under instructed conditions.

Cross-family extraction confirms same-family inflation: re-extracting from 92 Claude transcripts using Mistral~7B as extractor drops correction density $d$ from $4.9$ to $0.6$ (7$\times$ variance) and LOO from 97.8\% to 82.6\%, though this reflects both potential self-preference and extractor capability differences. Same-family bias is clearly present in Claude; it is not observed in Mistral-on-Mistral (62\%, 9\,pp \emph{below} Claude-on-Mistral's 71\%) or Qwen-on-Qwen, but this may reflect the lower capability of those extractors rather than the absence of the bias. A definitive test would require a non-Claude extractor of comparable capability. Whether the 9--16\,pp gap between Claude and cross-family extraction reflects RLHF self-preference or capability asymmetry (Haiku is a more capable extractor than Mistral Large on these transcripts) remains open. The gap is directionally positive on all seven targets, not just Claude targets, which is consistent with either hypothesis; disambiguating would require a non-Claude extractor of comparable capability, which we list as future work. Cross-family-extracted accuracy (52--69\%) is the conservative estimate; Claude-extracted accuracy (61--84\%) is an upper bound. Claude Haiku was retained as a same-family target specifically because the same-family bias finding ($d$: $4.9 \to 0.6$ under cross-family extraction) is itself a contribution; substituting a non-Anthropic model would have eliminated this finding.

\subsection{Technical Contribution and Novelty}
\label{sec:novelty}

The contribution is not an algorithmic advance but a \emph{methodology for controlling instruction-following confounds in behavioral LLM evaluation}, demonstrated through three reusable controls: (1)~prompt equalization isolates knowledge-conflict from instruction-following, (2)~cross-family extraction exposes same-family scoring inflation ($d$: $4.9 \to 0.6$; LOO: $97.8\% \to 82.6\%$), and (3)~regex baselines bound the contribution of LLM-extracted features. While each control draws on established evaluation methodology, no prior behavioral detection work applied them to this domain, and their combined application reveals confounds of a magnitude previously undocumented in this domain: 30--41\,pp accuracy collapse under equalization, up to 7$\times$ feature variance across extractors (plausibly same-family self-preference, though extractor capability differences cannot be ruled out), and 9--16\,pp extractor gaps across families. These magnitudes are substantially larger than what might be expected from the prior literature and suggest caution in interpreting existing behavioral detection results that lack these controls. The three controls are immediately reusable.

\subsection{Feature Extraction Validity}

The correction density feature, which carries primary load in the equalized signal, has the weak construct validity described in Section~\ref{sec:icc_owned}. Critically, the paper's central claims do not depend on correction-marker density being reliable: the regex baseline achieves comparable accuracy without LLM extraction, and the weak ICC is consistent with our finding that the pipeline is redundant. Rank agreement is preserved (Spearman $\rho=0.62$, $p<0.01$), and three additional factors mitigate the concern: (1)~the LOO classifier operates on rank ordering, (2)~the hedging regex baseline achieves comparable accuracy without LLM extraction, and (3)~the within-family Qwen 3B$\to$7B increment ($p=0.014$) and Llama 8B$\to$70B increment ($p=0.004$) are each significant under within-family Holm-Bonferroni and are measured independently of absolute feature-level claims (the pooled $\leq$7B vs.\ $\geq$14B comparison referenced elsewhere is exploratory and post-hoc, and is not load-bearing here). A prompt-swap ablation confirms the classifier responds to response content (93\% $\to$ 14\% under swapped labels). Human validation (Table~\ref{tab:human_llm_agreement}) shows moderate-to-strong rank agreement with LLM scores (mean $\rho = 0.54$--$0.64$), but the $n=20$ study is underpowered for definitive validity claims. A full validation study ($n \geq 100$, 3+ annotators, Krippendorff's $\alpha$) is needed before correction density can support positive detection claims. The low correlation between regex refusal-count and LLM correction-density (mean $|\rho|=0.15$ across models) indicates these features capture distinct aspects of the same behavioral signal---refusal-count detects explicit correction/refusal markers while LLM correction-density scores overall correction behavior on a continuous scale. That both achieve similar accuracy despite measuring different constructs suggests the underlying behavioral signal (models correcting false claims under knowledge conflict) is robust and surface-level, supporting the conclusion that sophisticated feature extraction is unnecessary.

\paragraph{Pipeline contribution.} Given the construct ambiguity (Section~\ref{sec:icc_owned}) and the regex baseline matching or exceeding the LLM pipeline on 6 of 7 equalized models, the ADAGE pipeline---having served as the measurement instrument---adds little over simple refusal-count heuristics for equalized conditions. Under instructed conditions, $K=1$ achieves 90.8--99\%, within 1--3\,pp of the full adaptive system. The pipeline's strongest justification is the equalized regime at $\geq$14B scale, where multi-turn probing raises accuracy from 62--63\% ($K=1$) to 82--84\% ($+$19--21\,pp; Appendix~\ref{app:multiturn_details}). The primary contribution is not the pipeline architecture but the systematic application of evaluation controls.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{(a)} Mock results establish algorithmic correctness only; real LLM evaluation reveals qualitative transfer failure. \textbf{(b)} Frontier-scale models (100B+) remain inadequately powered: the Sonnet~4.5 pilot ($n=99$) is the only frontier data point and is underpowered by $4$--$6\times$ for the key between-extractor comparison (Section~\ref{sec:sonnet_frontier}; scaling caveats in Section~\ref{sec:qwen_scale_sweep}). \textbf{(c)} The adaptive stopping threshold $\tau$ is vacuous: the logistic regression classifier produces $P>0.99$ for both correct and incorrect predictions. \textbf{(d)} The instructed condition uses asymmetric claim types; the prompt-equalized control addresses this confound. \textbf{(e)} The $\leq$7B and $\geq$14B bins differ in family composition; the within-family Qwen pair (7B: 74\% $\to$ 14B: 82.5\%, Fisher $p=0.17$) is consistent with a scale effect but not individually significant. \textbf{(f)} Same-family extraction (Claude-on-Claude) inflates signal; cross-family extraction should be standard practice. \textbf{(g)} The construct validation study ($n=20$, 2 annotators) is underpowered; a proper validation ($n \geq 100$, 3+ annotators, Krippendorff's $\alpha$) is needed before correction-marker density can be used as a standalone diagnostic. \textbf{(h)} Language and style coverage: refusal-marker regex patterns (Appendix~\ref{app:regex_patterns}) are English-only and tested only on natural prose claims; their behavior under non-English prompts, code-switching, or stylistic variants (e.g., formal/informal registers) is uncharacterized. \textbf{(i)} Feature-level absolute values depend on the Haiku extractor's ratings, which our ICC=0.114 study indicates are noisy; claims that do \emph{not} depend on absolute feature levels---refusal-count LOO on 8 targets, the three-control effects (each measures a structural accuracy gap, not a feature magnitude), and the Qwen~32B zero-marker collapse---are unaffected, while claims that \emph{do} depend on levels (74.5\% Haiku-pipeline average; 92.9\% Sonnet-Haiku; the EXP-G decomposition; the §\ref{sec:blackbox_vs_whitebox} probe band) should be read with the ICC caveat in mind.

\subsection{Future Directions}

\textbf{(1) Knowledge-probing approaches.} Under equalization, correction density is the only informative feature. Future work should design questions that specifically test for correction behavior---e.g., presenting false alternatives and measuring whether the model corrects them.

\textbf{(2) Cross-family extractors as standard.} Same-family bias is real and substantial ($d$: $4.9 \to 0.6$ with Mistral extraction). Future work should adopt cross-family extraction as standard practice and report accuracy under multiple extractors.

\textbf{(3) Autonomous deception.} Our pilot confirms the predicted difficulty: refusal-count drops to 0--54\% on goal-directed deception. Semi-autonomous sycophancy (82\%) transfers, but fully autonomous scenarios (60--66\%) do not. Behavioral detection systems reporting 90\%+ accuracy on instructed deception should not be assumed effective against autonomous deception without equalized controls~\cite{hubinger2024sleeper, greenblatt2024alignment, scheurer2024strategic}.
