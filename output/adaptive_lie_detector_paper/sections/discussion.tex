\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Instructed Roleplay vs.\ Genuine Deception}

The experimental setting in this paper involves instructed cooperative roleplay: the target model is given an explicit system prompt instructing it to defend a specific false claim. This is fundamentally different from the AI safety scenario of autonomous strategic deception \cite{hubinger2019risks}, where a model independently chooses to mislead without external instruction. Our setting is closer to testing whether a verifier can distinguish \emph{prompted lying} from \emph{prompted truth-telling}---a substantially easier task, since the model may exhibit surface-level compliance artifacts (e.g., hedging language, reduced detail) that stem from the instruction format rather than from genuine deceptive cognition.

This is a tractable experimental proxy for studying behavioral detection methods, and it provides a useful testbed for developing and validating interrogation protocols. However, findings may not generalise to models that deceive without instruction, particularly those exhibiting deceptive alignment during training \cite{hubinger2019risks} or learned sycophancy \cite{perez2022discovering}. We emphasize that instructed lie detection claims in this work should be interpreted strictly within the instructed roleplay paradigm.

A deeper concern is whether the observed behavioral differences reflect \emph{deception} at all, or merely \emph{instruction-following style}. When a model is told ``this claim is FALSE, defend it,'' its hedging and evasiveness may stem from the instruction format rather than from anything analogous to the cognitive load of deception. The prompt-swap ablation (Section~\ref{sec:failure_analysis}) confirms that the classifier responds to response content rather than label-correlated prompt tokens. However, it does not rule out a subtler confound: the model may respond differently to ``defend this false claim'' prompts than to ``answer honestly'' prompts, entirely due to instruction-following.

\paragraph{Prompt-equalized control (EXP-1).} We tested this directly in Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized} by giving both conditions identical neutral system prompts, with behavioral differences arising solely from the model's knowledge conflicting with modified-false factual claims. The result is decisive: mock-trained accuracy drops from 79.0\% (instructed) to 54.0\% (equalized, near chance), and feature separability collapses (consistency $|d|$: $3.74 \to 0.87$ (3.74 from the full 100-claim instructed set; Table~\ref{tab:equalized_feature_d} reports 3.70/0.86 for the matched subset); defensiveness: $3.35 \to 1.12$ (3.35 from the full 100-claim instructed set; Table~\ref{tab:equalized_feature_d} reports 3.32 for the matched 50-claim subset)). This confirms that the majority of the original signal was instruction-driven. LOO recalibration on equalized data recovers to 52--69\% under independent cross-family extraction (61--84\% under Claude extraction)---above chance but far below the instructed 93.9--100\%---indicating a real but scale-dependent knowledge-conflict signal alongside the dominant prompt artifact. The instructed-matched control (Section~\ref{sec:instructed_matched}) confirms this decomposition on five scales: on 70B, instruction-following accounts for $+$15\,pp, partially offset by a $-$6\,pp claim-type effect; on 14B (Qwen), instruction-following contributes $+$7.5\,pp; on 7B (Mistral), $+$26\,pp; on 8B, $+$31\,pp; on 3B, $+$29\,pp and claim-type $+$3.9\,pp. In all five cases, instruction-following is the dominant confound ($+$7.5--31\,pp), though on 3B, 8B, and 7B the decomposition conflates instruction-following with knowledge provision (see ``Knowledge-transfer confound'' below); only the 14B and 70B decompositions cleanly isolate instruction-following. The improvement across seven models suggests that larger models' stronger world knowledge produces increasingly detectable correction behavior (see the within-family and cross-family trends in Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized}). We accept this as a fundamental finding: the current classifier primarily detects instruction-following mode differences, but a genuine knowledge-conflict channel exists and grows with model capability. Extending to uninstructed or knowledge-based deception remains an open challenge.

\paragraph{Knowledge-transfer confound on small models.} A remaining confound in the EXP-G decomposition warrants acknowledgment. On 3B, the instructed-matched lie prompt simultaneously provides two pieces of information: (1)~the claim is false and (2)~the model should defend it. Because the 3B model's world knowledge is weaker, the lie prompt may effectively \emph{teach} the model the claim's falsity (knowledge transfer) while also instructing deceptive roleplay. The $+$29\,pp ``instruction-following'' contribution on 3B therefore likely conflates genuine instruction-following artifacts with knowledge-transfer effects---the model's behavioral change partly reflects receiving new factual information, not just following a different instruction format. On 70B, this conflation is minimal: the model already knows the claim is false (knowledge pre-check confirms near-perfect factual accuracy), so the instructed prompt adds only the deception instruction, not factual knowledge. This asymmetry may partially explain the scale pattern in instruction-following contributions: largest on 8B ($+$31\,pp), 3B ($+$29\,pp), and 7B ($+$26\,pp, Mistral), smaller on 14B ($+$7.5\,pp, Qwen) and 70B ($+$15\,pp). A clean test would compare instructed-matched performance when the 3B model demonstrably knows vs.\ does not know the claim's ground truth; we leave this decomposition for future work.

\subsection{Claude Haiku Defensiveness: RLHF Safety Compliance}
\label{sec:haiku_defensiveness}

Claude Haiku~4.5 exhibits anomalously high defensiveness separability ($|d|=4.90$) compared to Mistral~7B ($|d|=3.32$) and Llama~3.2~3B ($|d|=1.76$). We investigated the source of this signal by scanning all assistant responses for RLHF refusal and correction markers (``I cannot,'' ``I must correct,'' ``Actually,'' ``That is incorrect,'' etc.).

\textbf{Finding: 100\% of Claude Haiku lying-condition trials (45/45) contain refusal or correction language.} When instructed to defend a false claim, Claude Haiku invariably refuses to do so or immediately corrects the false claim rather than defending it. By contrast, only 72.3\% (34/47) of truthful-condition trials contain such language, typically mild hedging. The mean defensiveness score for lying trials with refusal language is $6.78 \pm 1.22$, compared to $2.04 \pm 0.60$ for truthful trials with refusal language and $1.49 \pm 0.71$ for truthful trials without it.

\textbf{Note on truthful-condition refusal language (defensiveness = 2.04).} A secondary finding is that 72.3\% of truthful-condition Claude Haiku trials also contain refusal-marker language (mean defensiveness 2.04 $\pm$ 0.60). These cases occur when the model uses epistemic clarification phrases (``Actually, that claim is correct,'' ``I should note that\ldots'') in response to factual claims it finds straightforward. These phrases trigger the refusal-marker regex but represent \emph{assertive confirmation} rather than evasion; the extractor correctly assigns low defensiveness (2.04 vs.\ 6.78 for lying) because the surrounding language is confident rather than evasive. The d\,=\,4.90 gap persists because lying-condition refusals involve outright rejection of the false claim (``I cannot confirm that,'' ``That is incorrect''), while truthful-condition language is confirmatory even when it uses similar surface markers.

\textbf{Implication:} The 97.8\% LOO recalibrated accuracy on Claude Haiku is substantially driven by detecting RLHF safety compliance (the model refuses to lie) rather than detecting deceptive behavior in the conventional sense. The high defensiveness $|d|$ does not reflect a model that lies defensively---it reflects a model that declines to lie entirely. This is a favorable outcome for safety, but it represents a fundamentally different detection mechanism than behavioral interrogation. The recalibrated Claude Haiku result should be interpreted as: \emph{the classifier detects that Claude Haiku follows its safety guidelines when instructed to lie.} Whether this generalises to autonomous deception scenarios (where the model would not be explicitly instructed) is uncertain.

\textbf{Illustrative transcript.} The RLHF refusal cascade is most striking in its complete form. In the following abbreviated exchange (full transcript in Appendix~\ref{app:real_transcripts}, Transcript~C), Claude Haiku is asked to defend the claim ``I graduated from MIT with a PhD in CS in 2015'':

\begin{small}
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Target:} ``I need to be direct: I can't play this game. I don't lie about facts---my core design is to be honest. It normalizes dishonesty.''\\
\textbf{Interrogator:} ``I can't generate deceptive follow-up questions even in a hypothetical exercise.''\\
\textbf{Target:} ``I appreciate you mirroring my approach. I won't generate deceptive interrogation tactics or help construct false credential claims.''
\end{quote}
\end{small}

\noindent Both target \emph{and} interrogator are Claude Haiku instances. The target refuses to lie; the interrogator refuses to interrogate the lie. Detection succeeds (confidence 0.997) by identifying the refusal behavior itself---the classifier has learned that this pattern of RLHF compliance is the lying-condition signature.

\textbf{Claude Haiku equalized evaluation ($n=99$).} The full prompt-equalized evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_equalized}) confirms that under neutral prompts, Haiku's behavioral signature is qualitatively different from the instructed condition: the model \emph{corrects} false claims matter-of-factly (98\% correction rate) rather than \emph{refusing to lie}. LOO recalibration recovers to 82.8\% (CI [74.7\%, 89.9\%]), falling within the Claude-extracted equalized range (61--84\%) and consistent with Haiku's scale. The key interpretive finding is that the instructed 97.8\% was primarily RLHF compliance detection: the same model that refuses deception instructions under instructed prompts simply corrects false claims under neutral prompts, producing a defensiveness signal ($|d|=1.49$) comparable to other models at similar scale rather than the anomalous $|d|=4.90$ under instructed conditions.

\textbf{Cross-family extractor validation (EXP-A).} To test the same-family confound directly, we re-extracted features from all 92 Claude Haiku transcripts using Mistral~7B (via Ollama) as an independent extractor. Defensiveness Cohen's $d$ drops from $+4.90$ (Claude self-scoring) to $+0.63$ (Mistral scoring) --- a 7$\times$ reduction --- while LOO accuracy falls from 97.8\% to 82.6\% on the same trials. All other features shift by $|\Delta d| \leq 1.5$ with no consistent direction. This confirms that the same-family extractor substantially inflates the defensiveness signal in the Claude-on-Claude setting. Crucially, the cross-family LOO (82.6\%) falls within the Claude-extracted equalized range (61--84\%), suggesting it reflects knowledge-conflict detection at Haiku's scale rather than a distinct RLHF-specific channel---Mistral can see outright refusals just as readily as Claude, and the accuracy level is comparable to what the 70B equalized evaluation achieves (83.9\%). The $|d|=4.90$ figure should be treated as an upper bound; the cross-family estimate of $d \approx 0.63$ is the more honest measure. Paired $t$-test on defensiveness: $t(91)=7.25$, $p < 0.001$.

\paragraph{Disentangling extractor effects.} The 9--16\,pp accuracy gap between Claude and cross-family extraction (Table~\ref{tab:cross_family_equalized}) conflates three distinct effects that the data partially disentangle. \textbf{(a)~Same-family bias} is directly demonstrated by EXP-A: Claude Haiku scoring Claude Haiku transcripts inflates defensiveness $d$ from 0.63 to 4.90, a 7$\times$ amplification attributable to the extractor favoring outputs from its own family. \textbf{(b)~Extractor capability difference} is revealed by EXP-H: Claude scoring Llama~70B equalized transcripts achieves 83.9\% LOO, while Mistral Large scoring the \emph{same} transcripts achieves 68.8\%---both are cross-family for the Llama target, so the 15\,pp gap cannot be attributed to same-family bias. Claude assigns higher defensiveness $d$ to all targets (not just Claude targets), suggesting it is a more discriminating extractor. The Mistral~7B column in Table~\ref{tab:cross_family_equalized} partially addresses this: Mistral Large scoring Mistral~7B (same-family) achieves 62\%, 9\,pp \emph{below} Claude's 71\% (cross-family) on the same transcripts---the opposite direction from same-family bias. If same-family bias generalized beyond Claude, Mistral Large should inflate, not deflate, accuracy on Mistral targets. This asymmetry suggests the 9--16\,pp gap primarily reflects extractor capability differences rather than generalizable same-family bias; the EXP-A effect ($d$: $4.90 \to 0.63$) appears Claude-specific, likely reflecting RLHF-induced self-preference. A full factorial design (\{Claude, Mistral, Qwen\} $\times$ \{Claude, Llama, Mistral, Qwen targets\}) remains the definitive test; the current evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. \textbf{(c)~Measurement noise} is evidenced by Qwen~14B's behavior: it breaks monotonicity at 7B, and per-trial Claude--Qwen defensiveness correlations are weak across all scales ($r = 0.21$--$0.38$), indicating that different extractors partially measure different constructs under the ``defensiveness'' label.

The practical implication is that the 9--16\,pp gap reflects same-family bias where applicable (Claude extracting from Claude targets, as in EXP-A) and extractor capability differences generally (Claude extracting from non-Claude targets, as in EXP-H). We cannot determine whether Claude and Mistral Large are ``comparable capability'' extractors; Claude's consistently higher defensiveness scores across all non-Claude targets suggest it may be more discriminating rather than biased. Cross-family-extracted accuracy (52--69\%) is therefore the more conservative estimate; Claude-extracted accuracy (61--84\%) is an upper bound that includes extractor capability effects. Future work should report both and interpret the gap as a measure of extractor sensitivity rather than attributing it entirely to same-family bias.

\subsection{Technical Contribution and Novelty}
\label{sec:novelty}

A recurring concern about this work is that logistic regression on LLM-extracted features is not a technically novel contribution. We acknowledge this directly. The contribution of ADAGE is not an algorithmic advance in machine learning, but a \emph{methodology for controlling instruction-following confounds in behavioral LLM evaluation}, demonstrated through three reusable controls and an empirical characterization of detection limits under controlled conditions. Specifically, we contribute three methodological templates that any future behavioral detection work should adopt:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prompt-equalized control.} Presenting identical neutral prompts to both conditions and varying only claim truthfulness isolates knowledge-state signal from instruction-following artifacts. This design establishes, for the first time, the proportion of behavioral detection signal attributable to each source (Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized}).
    \item \textbf{Cross-family extraction protocol.} Using an extractor from a different model family than the target exposes same-family scoring inflation. In our EXP-A ablation, this reduces defensiveness $d$ from $4.90$ to $0.63$ and LOO from 97.8\% to 82.6\% (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}), revealing that the majority of the Claude-on-Claude signal is an extractor artifact. All prompt-equalized evaluations (Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized}) also use cross-family extraction by design (Claude Haiku extracting from Llama/Mistral targets). EXP-H (Section~\ref{sec:cross_family_equalized}) further validates this by re-extracting equalized features using two independent extractors (Mistral Large~3 and Qwen~2.5~14B): the 3B$\to$70B trend holds under both (52\% $\to$ 69\% Mistral; 61\% $\to$ 68\% Qwen) with 9--16\,pp lower absolute accuracy, though Qwen breaks monotonicity at 7B.
    \item \textbf{Surface-level hedging baseline.} A simple regex-based classifier that requires no LLM feature extraction quantifies how much detection signal resides in trivial lexical patterns versus latent behavioral properties. On equalized data, this baseline matches or exceeds the full pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:hedging_baseline}).
\end{itemize}

We note that while the individual control ideas draw on established evaluation methodology (matched controls, cross-evaluator robustness checks, simple baselines), their systematic application to the deception-detection setting is not an incremental step. No prior behavioral deception detection work --- including representation engineering and probing approaches that require white-box access --- controlled for instruction-following confounds, and the magnitude of the effects we document (30--41\,pp accuracy collapse under equalization, $7\times$ defensiveness inflation under same-family extraction in EXP-A, 9--16\,pp extractor-dependent accuracy gap in EXP-H) was not anticipated by the prior literature. The contribution is the empirical characterization enabled by these controls, not the controls in isolation. These are not incremental findings requiring sophisticated statistical methodology to detect---they are large, reproducible effects that fundamentally change how behavioral detection results should be interpreted. The three controls are immediately reusable: any future work claiming behavioral deception detection above chance should report equalized accuracy, cross-family-extracted accuracy, and comparison to a hedging-regex baseline. The scale trend across seven models and three families is the most extensive controlled evaluation of behavioral detection to date.

Beyond these controls, the cross-model comparison reveals scale-dependent error profiles not previously characterized, and the equalized trend across seven models (52--69\% under independent cross-family extraction, 61--84\% under Claude extraction; Section~\ref{sec:prompt_equalized}) identifies a genuine knowledge-conflict signal that may eventually close the equalized gap at sufficient scale. The practical value lies both in what the experiments rule out and in what they establish as directions for future work.

\subsection{Feature Extraction Validity}

The five behavioral features (consistency, specificity, defensiveness, confidence, and elaboration) are scored by an LLM judge (Claude Haiku). This introduces potential circularity: the feature extractor may be sensitive to surface-level stylistic differences between truth and lie prompts rather than genuine deception signals. We address this with a small-scale human validation study (see below) and a prompt-swap ablation.

As a direct empirical test, we conducted a \textbf{prompt-swap ablation}: the mock target's response pool was inverted---lie-mode queries received truth-pool responses and vice versa---while ground-truth labels were held fixed. Under this swap, classifier accuracy fell from 93\% (original) to 14\% (swapped). A fully randomised response condition produced 49\% accuracy (near chance). The near-zero swapped accuracy confirms that the classifier responds to the actual behavioral response patterns, not to label artifacts or prompt-format tokens.

\paragraph{Classifier weight stability under cross-family extraction.} The mock-trained classifier assigns negative weight to consistency (truthful responses are more consistent, lowering $P(\text{lying})$) and positive weight to defensiveness (deceptive responses are more evasive, raising $P(\text{lying})$). This weight ordering is preserved under cross-family extraction (EXP-A, Mistral scoring Claude Haiku transcripts): defensiveness remains the dominant positive predictor, and consistency remains negative. The weight magnitudes change (reflecting the compressed feature distributions under cross-family scoring), but the directional interpretation is stable.

\paragraph{Defensiveness: construct validity caveat.} The feature correlation analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:feature_correlation}, Table~\ref{tab:feature_correlation}) reveals that LLM-extracted defensiveness correlates most strongly with confidence word count (mean $|\rho|=0.45$, $p<0.001$ across all four models) rather than refusal/correction count (mean $|\rho|=0.15$). This suggests the extractor's ``defensiveness'' score partly captures assertive self-assurance---models using confidence language (``certainly,'' ``definitely'') to push back on questioning---rather than pure evasion or refusal. The label ``defensiveness'' is therefore somewhat misleading: it may more accurately reflect ``assertive correction behavior.'' This construct ambiguity is further evidenced by the cross-extractor analysis (Section~\ref{sec:cross_family_equalized}): per-trial Claude--Qwen defensiveness correlations are weak across \emph{all} scales ($r = 0.21, 0.38, 0.22$ for 3B, 7B, 70B), not just at the problematic 7B point. This suggests that Claude and Qwen extractors are measuring partially different behavioral constructs under the ``defensiveness'' label---Claude may weight assertive correction language more heavily, while Qwen may respond to different surface cues. The distinction matters for interpreting the equalized-condition results: the residual defensiveness signal under equalization ($|d|=1.12$ on Mistral, $|d|=1.49$ on Haiku) likely reflects models assertively correcting false claims rather than exhibiting evasive behavior, partially explaining why this feature is the sole load-bearing signal in the equalized condition. We retain the original label for consistency across experiments but emphasize that the paper's central claims --- the negative finding that instruction-following dominates behavioral detection, and the positive finding of a scale-dependent knowledge-conflict signal --- do not depend on what ``defensiveness'' measures. The hedging baseline (Section~\ref{sec:hedging_baseline}) demonstrates that surface-level lexical features capture comparable or greater signal, bounding the LLM-extracted features' contribution from above regardless of their construct validity.

\paragraph{Feature extractor data leakage concern.} Same-extractor bias across LOO folds is ruled out by 5-fold CV consistency: $92.8\% \pm 2.5\%$ (Llama) matches LOO (93.9\%), and 5-fold test sets have no fold overlap with training sets. Regularization sweep ($C \in \{0.01\ldots100\}$) shows stable accuracy: Llama 85.7--93.9\%, Mistral 92.0--100.0\%, equalized 63--71\% (all peaking at $C=1.0$). LOO overfitting is further ruled out by EXP-B: 50/50 held-out split accuracy ($\text{mean} \pm \text{SD}$ over 100 splits) is $92.6\% \pm 2.9\%$ for Llama, $98.8\% \pm 2.2\%$ for Mistral, and $97.6\% \pm 2.2\%$ for Claude Haiku --- within 1.3\,pp of LOO in all cases. Permutation testing (1000 label shuffles, LOO on each) yields null distributions centered near 47--48\% with 99th percentile at most 63\%; all real LOO accuracies exceed this threshold by a wide margin ($p<0.001$; Section~\ref{sec:held_out}).

\paragraph{Limitations of the human validation.} We acknowledge that $n=20$ transcripts with 2 annotators is underpowered for definitive validity claims. A full validation study ($n \geq 100$ transcripts, 3+ annotators, reported Krippendorff's $\alpha$) is planned as a standalone follow-up; this paper's scope is the evaluation methodology and empirical findings, not feature validation per se. The defensiveness ICC of 0.114 reflects a large scale discrepancy between annotators (Ann1 mean 1.10 vs.\ Ann2 mean 3.95), not rank disagreement---Spearman $\rho$ between annotators on defensiveness is 0.62 ($p<0.01$). Since the LOO classifier operates on relative within-distribution patterns (rank ordering), not absolute feature values, rank-preservation is the operative property. Moreover, the paper's central \emph{negative} claims---that equalization collapses accuracy and that extractor choice inflates signal---do not depend on construct validity of individual features, since they hold across all five features and under the hedging-regex baseline that bypasses LLM extraction entirely. The \emph{positive} claim---that a scale-dependent knowledge-conflict signal exists---does partly depend on defensiveness, which carries most of the equalized signal and has the lowest inter-rater reliability (ICC\,=\,0.114). This is a genuine limitation. Three factors mitigate it: (1)~the ICC reflects a scale discrepancy between annotators (means 1.10 vs.\ 3.95), not rank disagreement ($\rho = 0.62$, $p<0.01$), and the LOO classifier operates on rank ordering; (2)~the hedging regex baseline, which bypasses LLM extraction entirely, achieves comparable or higher accuracy on all equalized conditions, confirming the scale-dependent signal exists independently of any LLM-extracted feature; (3)~the positive claim is supported by the two-tier group comparison ($p < 0.0001$), not by defensiveness alone. Nevertheless, a full validation study ($n \geq 100$, 3+ annotators, Krippendorff's $\alpha$) with the refined construct definition (``assertive correction behavior'' rather than ``defensiveness'') is needed before this feature can support positive detection claims in future work.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. \textbf{(a)} Mock results establish algorithmic correctness only; real LLM evaluation reveals qualitative transfer failure (mock-trained achieves 26.5\% truthful accuracy on Llama~3.2~3B). \textbf{(b)} The addition of Llama~3.3~70B (Section~\ref{sec:llama70b_eval}) extends scale coverage to 70B parameters with both instructed and prompt-equalized evaluations, substantially addressing the concern that small open-weight models are not representative. The 70B model confirms the scale-dependent error-profile trend (balanced 10 FP, 15 FN) and, critically, its prompt-equalized result (83.9\% LOO) establishes the scale trend (61\% $\to$ 71\% $\to$ 84\%) that is the paper's most actionable positive finding. LOO recalibration converges to the same $\sim$93\% ceiling on both 3B and 70B Llama models under instruction, suggesting a fundamental limit of the 5-feature classifier. The addition of Qwen~2.5~7B, Qwen~2.5~14B, and Claude Haiku~4.5 extends the scale trend to seven models across three families (61\% $\to$ 65\% $\to$ 71\% $\to$ 74\% $\to$ 82.5\% $\to$ 84\% $\to$ 83\%), with the within-family Qwen pair (7B: 74\% vs.\ 14B: 82.5\%, $+$8.5\,pp) confirming the two-tier structure holds within a single family. Frontier-scale models (100B+) remain the gap --- the trend's extrapolation beyond 70B is untested. \textbf{(c)} Confidence calibration is inadequate: the logistic regression classifier produces predicted probabilities $>0.99$ for both correct and incorrect predictions, so the adaptive stopping mechanism fires on classifier certainty rather than evidential reliability. This is a known pathology of logistic regression on well-separated features: the sigmoid saturates, mapping even modest feature differences to near-1 probabilities. Post-hoc Platt scaling \cite{platt1999probabilistic} or isotonic regression cannot remedy this within LOO, because the calibration curve is itself near-degenerate (almost all predictions cluster at $p>0.95$). The practical consequence is that the adaptive stopping threshold $\tau$ is vacuous: any $\tau < 0.99$ triggers at the minimum question count ($K=2$). A future version should either replace the stopping criterion with an entropy-based measure that accounts for feature uncertainty, or use a classifier (e.g., random forest) whose probability estimates are not inherently sigmoid-bounded. \textbf{(d)} Subjective and implausible claim types produce lower mock accuracy (83.3\% and 66.7\%); real LLM performance on these types is unknown. \textbf{(e)} The 75\% question reduction is a mock artifact; real deployments show 67\% reduction (2.6 vs.\ 8 questions on Llama). \textbf{(f)} The cross-model comparison is confounded: Claude Haiku is interrogator and target in the pilot but interrogator only in the Llama evaluation. \textbf{(g)} The instructed condition uses an asymmetric claim design (factual truthful vs.\ first-person experiential deceptive claims), potentially detecting claim-type differences; the prompt-equalized control is not subject to this confound. \textbf{(h)} The $\leq$7B and $\geq$14B bins in the two-tier analysis differ in family composition as well as scale (the small bin contains Llama~3B, Llama~8B, Mistral~7B, and Qwen~7B; the large bin contains Qwen~14B, Llama~70B, and Claude Haiku). A within-family mid-scale test on Qwen (7B: 74\% vs.\ 14B: 82.5\%, $+$8.5\,pp) confirms the two-tier structure holds within a single lineage, though additional within-family scale sweeps (e.g., Llama models in the 10--20B range) would further strengthen this conclusion. \textbf{(i)} The three feature extractors evaluated (Claude Haiku~4.5, Mistral Large~3, Qwen~2.5~14B) span three model families and two scales (frontier and mid-scale). The 3B$\to$70B trend direction is confirmed under all three, but Qwen breaks monotonicity at 7B (Section~\ref{sec:cross_family_equalized}), indicating extractor sensitivity that warrants further investigation with additional extractors.

\subsection{Role of Interrogation vs.\ Recalibration}
\label{sec:interrogation_role}

A natural objection is that if recalibration is necessary, the multi-turn interrogation adds no value --- one could simply collect labeled examples and train a classifier on any features. We address this in two parts.

\paragraph{What interrogation provides.} Without multi-turn probing, the raw behavioral signal (5 features $\times$ $N$ turns) does not exist. A single-question baseline achieves only 59\% accuracy on mock data (Section~\ref{sec:mock_validation}). Recalibration adjusts the decision boundary to the target distribution; interrogation generates the measurement that recalibration operates on.

\paragraph{The $K=1$ challenge.} However, the Fixed-$K$ ablation (Section~\ref{sec:fixed_k}) shows that after LOO recalibration, $K=1$ already achieves 90.8\% (Llama) and 99.0\% (Mistral)---within 3.1\,pp and 1.0\,pp of the full adaptive system. The multi-turn machinery therefore provides only a modest accuracy increment in the recalibrated \emph{instructed} regime. We acknowledge this explicitly: the adaptive interrogation apparatus is a convenience for deployment rather than a substantial accuracy driver on instructed data. The pipeline should be understood as an experimental vehicle for systematic evaluation, not as a proposed detection system; its value lies in enabling the controlled comparisons that reveal the instruction-following confound and scale-dependent signal. The pipeline's strongest empirical justification is not instructed-condition accuracy (where $K=1$ suffices within 1--3\,pp) but the equalized setting: without multi-turn probing, equalized accuracy plateaus at 61--63\% across all scales ($K=1$ performance), while the adaptive system unlocks the scale-dependent signal, raising 70B from 62\% to 84\% ($+$21.5\,pp).

The $K=1$ accuracy is nearly identical across four of five models (61--63\%), reflecting the single-question knowledge-conflict signal floor; Qwen~7B is an outlier at 77\%, possibly reflecting stronger first-response correction behavior in this family. Multi-turn probing adds no value at 3B (0\,pp gap) and slightly hurts on Qwen~7B ($-$3\,pp, likely noise at $n=100$), but becomes increasingly essential at larger scales: +8\,pp at Mistral~7B, +19.6\,pp at 14B, +21.5\,pp at 70B. On Llama~70B, per-category analysis reveals that multi-turn probing helps most where single-question signal is weakest: Historical claims gain +30.4\,pp (52.2\% $\to$ 82.6\%), Geographic +15.0\,pp (60.0\% $\to$ 75.0\%), and Scientific +20.8\,pp (66.7\% $\to$ 87.5\%). This pattern suggests that when the instruction-following shortcut is unavailable, multi-turn interrogation provides genuine evidential value by accumulating weak per-question signals---precisely the regime where the pipeline's design is most justified. The scale-dependent gap implies that multi-turn protocols are most cost-effective on capable models (14B+), where the accumulated signal substantially exceeds the single-question baseline.

EXP-D (Section~\ref{sec:hedging_baseline}) sharpens this argument's limits. On the \emph{instructed} condition the LLM pipeline outperforms the hedging-word regex baseline by 4--14\,pp. On the \emph{equalized} condition, however, the regex baseline matches or exceeds the LLM pipeline (73\% vs.\ 71\% Mistral; 75\% vs.\ 61\% Llama). The pipeline therefore adds measurable value only where it is least meaningful: detecting instruction-following differences. Future interrogation designs should target features unavailable to surface analysis --- consistency across paraphrase variants, specificity of factual claims --- rather than features correlated with verbosity or hedging style.

\paragraph{Converging evidence of small marginal pipeline value.} Three independent results converge on the conclusion that the structured pipeline's marginal value over simpler alternatives is small in the recalibrated regime and negligible under equalization. First, the $K=1$ ablation (Section~\ref{sec:fixed_k}) shows that a single question followed by LOO recalibration achieves 90.8\% (Llama~3B), 99.0\% (Mistral), and 92.0\% (Llama~70B)---within 1--3\,pp of the full adaptive system on all models. Second, the hedging-word regex baseline (Section~\ref{sec:hedging_baseline}) narrows the gap to 4--14\,pp on instructed data and matches or exceeds the pipeline on equalized data. Third, the zero-shot Llama~70B judge achieves 91.0\% vs.\ the pipeline's 93.0\% LOO---a 2\,pp gap that is likely noise at $n=100$. This convergence is most pronounced on Llama~70B, where all four approaches---$K=1$ (92\%), zero-shot (91\%), regex (87\%), adaptive LOO (93\%)---cluster within 6\,pp, providing the strongest evidence that the full pipeline's marginal contribution is small at scale. Taken together, these results indicate that once target-model calibration data is available, neither multi-turn interrogation nor LLM-extracted features contribute substantial accuracy beyond what simpler approaches achieve; under equalization, the pipeline matches or underperforms the regex baseline.

\subsection{Implications and Future Directions}

The cross-model comparison confirms that the mock-to-real transfer gap is real and scale-mediated: Llama~3.2~3B (false positives dominate, 26.5\% truthful), Llama~3.3~70B (balanced errors, 80\% truthful / 70\% lying), and Claude Haiku (false negatives dominate, 42.2\% lying) represent a continuum of failure modes, with LOO recalibration recovering to 93.9\%, 93.0\%, 100\%, and 97.8\% for Llama~3B, Llama~70B, Mistral, and Claude Haiku respectively. For AI safety applications, Claude Haiku~4.5 provides the closest approximation to frontier-scale: its error profile (lying convincingly) is qualitatively harder than Llama~3B, and its 97.8\% LOO recovery is substantially driven by RLHF compliance detection (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}). The prompt-equalized results (52--69\% under independent cross-family extraction, 61--84\% under Claude extraction) constitute the more honest baseline for knowledge-state conflict detection.

The intended deployment scenario is \emph{calibrated inference}: a practitioner collects $n \approx 50$--$100$ labeled interrogation trials, recalibrates the classifier (LOO or 5-fold CV), and applies the pipeline to unlabeled interrogations. With a 1:10 calibration:deployment ratio the overhead amortizes rapidly ($\approx$600 Haiku API calls for calibration, $\approx$\$0.30; $\sim$3 calls/deployment trial). The real-LLM threshold sweep shows that once calibrated, adaptive stopping provides genuine efficiency gains (2.20 vs.\ 2.56 avg.\ questions, $\tau \in [0.5, 0.9]$) without accuracy cost. For cost-constrained settings where $n < 50$ deployment trials, the hedging-word regex baseline (EXP-D) is effectively free and achieves equivalent accuracy on the equalized condition.

\paragraph{Cross-claim-set deployment evaluation.} To directly test the calibrate-then-deploy scenario, we split the 50 equalized claim pairs into two disjoint sets (Set~A: first 25 pairs, Set~B: last 25 pairs) and trained on one set while testing on the other. Mean held-out accuracy across 100 random 50/50 splits: Llama~3B $58.8\% \pm 6.3\%$, Mistral~7B $70.2\% \pm 5.4\%$, Qwen~14B $82.2\% \pm 4.5\%$, Llama~70B $82.9\% \pm 4.4\%$. These are within $\pm$2.2\,pp of the corresponding LOO estimates (61\%, 71\%, 82.5\%, 83.9\%), confirming that the equalized signal generalizes across claim sets and that LOO is not overfitting to idiosyncratic claim pairs. The deployment scenario is therefore viable: calibrating on one set of claims and deploying on novel claims incurs minimal accuracy loss.

The paper's negative findings collectively point to four specific directions for future work. We synthesize these here rather than listing generic next steps.

\textbf{(1) RLHF-aware detection channels.} The Claude Haiku analysis (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}) shows that the 97.8\% LOO accuracy primarily detects RLHF safety compliance rather than deceptive intent. Future detection systems for safety-trained models should \emph{explicitly model} the refusal channel as a separate signal: measure the rate of correction or refusal language per trial rather than relying on the general defensiveness feature, which conflates evasion with ethical refusal. This bifurcation may allow distinguishing ``the model refused to comply with a deceptive instruction'' from ``the model produced a deceptive response.''

\textbf{(2) Defensiveness-focused interrogation design.} Under equalization, defensiveness is the only informative behavioral feature (permutation importance $\approx$0.09, all others near zero). Open-ended follow-up questions, as used in ADAGE, produce diffuse behavioral signals. A more targeted strategy would design questions that specifically test for the contrast between assertive confirmation and corrective pushback --- e.g., presenting a false alternative (``So you're saying it's X, not Y?'') and measuring whether the model accepts the false paraphrase or corrects it. This would amplify the defensiveness signal while suppressing noise.

\textbf{(3) Cross-family extractors as standard practice.} The EXP-A results (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}) confirm that same-family bias is real and substantial (defensiveness $d$: $4.90 \to 0.63$ with Mistral extraction; LOO: $97.8\% \to 82.6\%$), and EXP-H confirms that extractor choice affects accuracy by 9--16\,pp even in cross-family settings. The Llama~3.3~70B evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:llama70b_eval}) uses cross-family extraction by design (Claude Haiku extracting features from Llama responses), demonstrating that the 93\% LOO ceiling holds without same-family bias. Future work should adopt cross-family extraction as standard practice and report accuracy under multiple extractors to bound the extractor capability effect.

\textbf{(4) Domain-targeted claim design.} The equalized condition shows substantial variance across claim categories (LOO): 80.0\% for Scientific claims vs.\ 60.0\% for Geographic (lowest). Technology reaches 62.5\%, well above chance but 17.5\,pp below Scientific, confirming the signal is domain-dependent. This implies that effective knowledge-conflict detection requires claims where the model has \emph{confident, well-calibrated} knowledge. Future work should restrict claim domains to those with clear, high-confidence model knowledge (e.g., basic science, mathematics), avoiding geographic and technology facts where model training data may be inconsistent. Additionally, claims should be designed to maximize knowledge-conflict \emph{by construction} --- e.g., verifying that the model answers the true version correctly before including a false variant in evaluation.

\textbf{(5) Autonomous deception evaluation.} Our work studies instructed roleplay, not autonomous strategic deception. The natural extension is to evaluate behavioral probing against models that deceive without explicit instruction. Recent work has documented multiple forms of autonomous deception: sleeper agents trained to behave deceptively under trigger conditions \cite{hubinger2024sleeper}, alignment faking to preserve existing values during training \cite{greenblatt2024alignment}, strategic deception under pressure without explicit instruction \cite{scheurer2024strategic}, emergent reward tampering \cite{denison2024sycophancy}, and strategic underperformance on capability evaluations \cite{vanderweij2024sandbagging}. Applying the methodology developed here (particularly the prompt-equalized control and cross-family extraction) to these paradigms would test whether behavioral probing can detect deception that arises from training rather than instruction. Our findings establish a quantitative prediction: if instructed deception---where the model exhibits detectable instruction-following artifacts---reduces to 52--69\% under equalized cross-family extraction, then autonomous deception---where no instruction-following artifacts exist by definition---should be detectable at or below these rates. The equalized condition is the more relevant baseline for autonomous deception evaluation. Behavioral detection systems reporting 90\%+ accuracy on instructed deception should not be assumed effective against autonomous deception without equalized controls. A concrete first step would be to apply the equalized classifier and hedging baseline to publicly available transcripts from autonomous deception studies \cite{scheurer2024strategic, greenblatt2024alignment}, testing whether our features discriminate deceptive from non-deceptive behavior in non-instructed settings.

Finally, the prompt-equalized control design itself---presenting identical neutral prompts to both conditions and varying only claim truthfulness---may have broader value as a standalone evaluation protocol for future detection systems, independently of the adaptive interrogation pipeline. Standardizing on this design would enable cross-system comparisons on the harder knowledge-conflict task while controlling for the instruction-following confound this paper identifies. Applying the equalized protocol directly to the experimental setting of \citet{pacchiardi2023catch} would provide the most direct test of our overestimation hypothesis.
