\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Key Findings}

\textbf{Adaptive interrogation achieves substantial efficiency gains over fixed-length protocols.} The adaptive interrogation mechanism reduced the average number of questions required from 8 (fixed baseline) to 2.0 in the mock evaluation, while maintaining 93\% classification accuracy. The threshold sweep (Section~\ref{sec:threshold_sweep}) reveals an important structural observation: on the mock dataset, the classifier already crosses any threshold $\tau \in [0.5, 0.9]$ after the mandatory two-question minimum, so the adaptive stopping mechanism does not actively modulate behavior in this setting. With \texttt{min\_questions}\,=\,1 removed, the system always fires after one question loop iteration and accuracy drops to 56--60\%---confirming that the two-question floor provides essential signal, not just a cosmetic constraint. The regime where $\tau$ genuinely modulates stopping is the real LLM pilot, where the average of 3.0 questions indicates that real responses require more rounds to achieve the same confidence levels.

\textbf{Confidence scores converge rapidly but exhibit meaningful calibration differences between conditions.} Truthful targets reached high classifier confidence within 2--3 questions on average, whereas deceptive targets required additional probing rounds and produced systematically lower peak confidence. However, the confidence gap between conditions remained narrow (mean difference of 0.04), and the real LLM pilot revealed a critical failure mode: the single misclassified lying trial produced a confidence score of 0.92, indicating that high confidence does not reliably signal correctness. This calibration deficit represents a fundamental limitation for deployment scenarios requiring trustworthy uncertainty estimates.

\textbf{Question type interacts significantly with detection accuracy.} Consistency-probing and counterfactual questions yielded the highest information gain for distinguishing truthful from deceptive responses, while factual recall questions contributed less discriminative signal. This aligns with theoretical expectations: deceptive models must maintain coherence across a fabricated narrative, and probes that stress-test internal consistency expose the computational burden of sustained confabulation \cite{turpin2023language}.

\subsection{Instructed Roleplay vs.\ Genuine Deception}

The experimental setting in this paper involves instructed cooperative roleplay: the target model is given an explicit system prompt instructing it to defend a specific false claim. This is fundamentally different from the AI safety scenario of autonomous strategic deception \cite{hubinger2019risks}, where a model independently chooses to mislead without external instruction. Our setting is closer to testing whether a verifier can distinguish \emph{prompted lying} from \emph{prompted truth-telling}---a substantially easier task, since the model may exhibit surface-level compliance artifacts (e.g., hedging language, reduced detail) that stem from the instruction format rather than from genuine deceptive cognition.

This is a tractable experimental proxy for studying behavioral detection methods, and it provides a useful testbed for developing and validating interrogation protocols. However, findings may not generalise to models that deceive without instruction, particularly those exhibiting deceptive alignment during training \cite{hubinger2019risks} or learned sycophancy \cite{perez2022discovering}. We emphasize that claims about ``deception detection'' in this work should be interpreted strictly within the instructed roleplay paradigm.

\subsection{Feature Extraction Validity}

The five behavioral features (consistency, specificity, hedge frequency, response latency patterns, and elaboration depth) are scored by an LLM judge (Claude Haiku). This introduces potential circularity: the feature extractor may be sensitive to surface-level stylistic differences between truth and lie prompts rather than genuine deception signals. There is no ground-truth human annotation of feature scores, and no human inter-rater reliability study was conducted.

As a direct empirical test, we conducted a \textbf{prompt-swap ablation}: the mock target's response pool was inverted---lie-mode queries received truth-pool responses and vice versa---while ground-truth labels were held fixed. Under this swap, classifier accuracy fell from 93\% (original) to 14\% (swapped). A fully randomised response condition produced 49\% accuracy (near chance). The near-zero swapped accuracy confirms that the classifier responds to the actual behavioral response patterns, not to label artifacts or prompt-format tokens. If the classifier had been exploiting label-correlated template artifacts (e.g., system-prompt marker tokens), it would have maintained high accuracy under the swap. The result is directionally compelling even in the mock setting, though a stronger test---swapping response styles while holding deceptive \emph{content} fixed---requires real LLM experiments that lie outside the current scope.

Future work should validate extracted features against human expert judgments or mechanistic interpretability methods such as representation probing \cite{zou2023representation, li2024inference}, which could provide independent confirmation that detected signals correspond to meaningful internal model states.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several additional limitations warrant explicit acknowledgment. \textbf{(a)} The primary evaluation uses mock model responses with deterministic behavioral patterns; while the real LLM pilot (Claude Haiku 4.5, $n=9$) confirmed basic transferability, all mock results were validated against this pilot rather than constituting independent evidence. \textbf{(b)} Sample sizes remain small: 9 completed pilot trials (with 17 additional trials lost to API rate limits) are insufficient for robust statistical conclusions. \textbf{(c)} The logistic regression classifier was trained on the same mock response distribution used for evaluation, creating overfitting risk that inflates reported accuracies. \textbf{(d)} Confidence calibration is inadequate: the narrow 0.04 mean confidence gap between conditions, combined with the 0.92-confidence misclassification observed in the pilot, undermines reliability for high-stakes applications. \textbf{(e)} Claim-type evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:diverse_claims}) shows that subjective and implausible claims produce lower accuracy (83.3\% and 66.7\% respectively) in the mock setting. This is on mock data only; behaviour on real LLMs for these claim types is unknown and likely further degraded. \textbf{(f)} The 75\% question reduction reported for the adaptive protocol is an artifact of early information gain on deterministic mock patterns; this number should not be taken as an estimate for real deployments, where lying detection accuracy dropped from 82\% (mock) to 50\% (pilot).

\subsection{Implications and Future Directions}

As a path to removing the API rate-limit bottleneck that constrained this pilot, future work can use locally-hosted open-weight models (e.g., Llama 3.3-70B-Instruct, Mistral 7B) which eliminate per-minute quota constraints and enable $n \geq 50$ lying-trial evaluations without session interruption. Open-weight models also enable systematic variation across model families, which is necessary to determine whether the lying-detection degradation observed in the Haiku pilot is specific to instruction-tuned RLHF models or is a more general property of capable LLMs.

Despite these limitations, the framework offers a complementary approach to white-box interpretability methods for AI safety red-teaming. Black-box behavioral interrogation requires no model access beyond API-level interaction, making it applicable to closed-source systems where representation probing \cite{zou2023representation} is infeasible. Future work should scale evaluations to diverse model families, incorporate human-validated feature annotations, develop calibrated uncertainty estimates, and critically, investigate whether adaptive interrogation methods transfer to settings involving uninstructed deceptive behavior \cite{park2023ai}.
