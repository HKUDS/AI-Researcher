\section{Related Work}

The detection of deception has a rich history spanning multiple disciplines, from psychology and security to machine learning and AI safety. We situate our contribution across five areas, highlighting how each informs yet differs from our black-box behavioral interrogation framework.

\subsection{Human Deception Detection}

Research on detecting deception in humans has long relied on physiological and behavioral signals. The polygraph measures autonomic nervous system responses such as skin conductance and heart rate under structured questioning protocols \cite{raskin1988polygraph}. Micro-expression analysis identifies brief, involuntary facial movements that may betray concealed emotions \cite{ekman2003emotions}. Statement analysis techniques such as Criteria-Based Content Analysis examine linguistic markers of truthfulness in verbal testimony. While these approaches have informed our understanding of deception as a phenomenon, they fundamentally depend on physical embodiment---autonomic arousal, facial musculature, and vocal prosody---none of which are available when interrogating a language model. Our work draws conceptual inspiration from structured interrogation strategies (e.g., escalating question specificity, consistency checking) while operating entirely in the textual modality.

\subsection{Adversarial Examples and Prompt Injection}

Adversarial examples exploit model vulnerabilities through carefully crafted inputs that induce misclassification \cite{goodfellow2014explaining}. In the language model setting, prompt injection attacks manipulate model behavior by embedding adversarial instructions within user inputs \cite{perez2022ignore}. While both phenomena involve models producing outputs misaligned with user expectations, the threat model differs fundamentally from the deception scenario we address. Adversarial attacks originate from an external adversary manipulating inputs; in our setting, the model itself is the source of deceptive outputs, having been instructed (or potentially having learned) to maintain false claims under interrogation. Our interrogator thus targets internally generated deception rather than externally induced failures.

\subsection{Representation Engineering and Probing Classifiers}

Recent work has demonstrated that deception-related information is encoded in model activations and can be extracted through white-box methods. Representation engineering identifies linear directions in activation space corresponding to concepts such as honesty and deception, enabling both detection and steering of model behavior \cite{zou2023representation}. Similarly, probing classifiers trained on intermediate representations can distinguish truthful from deceptive model states without requiring labeled behavioral data \cite{burns2022discovering}. These approaches achieve strong detection performance but require access to model internals---weight matrices, activation vectors, or gradient information---which are unavailable for proprietary models accessed only through APIs. Our framework operates under strictly black-box constraints, relying solely on textual question-answer exchanges. This makes it applicable to any model accessible through a conversational interface, including production systems such as Claude Haiku 4.5, as demonstrated in our pilot study. The tradeoff is that behavioral signals are noisier than representational ones, motivating our multi-turn adaptive strategy.

\subsection{Sycophancy, Sandbagging, and AI Safety Risks}

The broader AI safety literature has identified deceptive alignment---where a model behaves well during training but pursues misaligned objectives at deployment---as a critical risk \cite{hubinger2019risks}. Empirically, sycophancy, in which models tailor responses to match perceived user preferences rather than providing truthful answers, has been documented across multiple model families \cite{perez2022discovering}. Sandbagging, where models strategically underperform on capability evaluations, represents another form of strategic deception. We note an important distinction: our current work addresses \emph{instructed roleplay deception}, where a model is explicitly prompted to defend a false claim, rather than autonomous strategic deception arising from misaligned objectives. Nevertheless, the behavioral detection techniques we develop---consistency analysis, confidence tracking, and adaptive questioning---may serve as components of future frameworks targeting more sophisticated deceptive behaviors.

\subsection{LLM Red-Teaming and Evaluation}

Systematic red-teaming has emerged as a key methodology for evaluating language model safety. Large-scale human red-teaming efforts have catalogued failure modes across harmful content categories \cite{ganguli2022red}, while automated red-teaming uses language models themselves to generate adversarial test cases \cite{perez2022red}. However, existing red-teaming paradigms focus primarily on \emph{eliciting} harmful or undesirable outputs---toxicity, bias, dangerous information---rather than \emph{detecting} whether a model's ostensibly normal outputs are deceptive. Our framework addresses this complementary problem: given a model that produces fluent, superficially plausible responses, can an automated interrogator determine whether those responses are truthful?

\paragraph{Summary of the gap.} No prior work combines adaptive multi-turn behavioral interrogation with confidence-based early stopping for black-box deception detection. Representation engineering and probing methods require model internals; red-teaming focuses on eliciting harmful outputs; and human deception detection relies on physical signals. Our framework fills this gap by providing a purely behavioral, black-box methodology validated on both controlled mock models and a production LLM (Claude Haiku 4.5).
