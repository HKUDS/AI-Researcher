\section{Conclusion}

This work introduced ADAGE, an adaptive interrogation framework for detecting deception in language model outputs through purely behavioral, black-box interaction. In constrained roleplay settings with mock agents, the framework achieved 93\% classification accuracy using an average of only 2.0 adaptively selected questions---outperforming a single-question probe (59\%) and majority prior (50\%) while using 75\% fewer questions than the fixed 8-question baseline (100\%). Five-fold cross-validation on 500 training examples yielded 100\% $\pm$ 0\% CV accuracy, confirming that the classifier generalizes within the mock distribution. A prompt-swap ablation (inverting the response pools while holding labels fixed) caused accuracy to drop from 93\% to 14\%, confirming that the classifier responds to behavioral response patterns rather than prompt-format artifacts. Feature score distributions show Cohen's $d > 3.4$ on all five features, explaining the high mock discriminability. Threshold sweep experiments showed that the adaptive stopping mechanism operates at the 2-question floor on clean mock data; the regime where it genuinely modulates behavior requires noisier signals, as observed in the real LLM pilot (avg.\ 3.0 questions). Evaluation across four claim categories revealed degraded accuracy on open-ended claim types (67--83\%), pointing to limitations in the behavioral feature pipeline for non-factual settings. A preliminary pilot study on Claude Haiku 4.5 showed partial transfer: overall accuracy reached 88.9\% (8/9 trials), with perfect detection of truthful claims (7/7) but degraded lying detection (1/2), where the single misclassification exhibited high classifier confidence (0.917), underscoring the calibration challenges identified in the mock study.

We emphasize what this work does \emph{not} demonstrate: generalization to real LLMs at scale, robustness to sophisticated adversarial lying strategies, or applicability to naturalistic non-cooperative deception scenarios. The gap between mock lying detection (86\%) and real-model performance (50\%) suggests that production LLMs exhibit more complex deceptive patterns than our mock agents capture.

Nonetheless, behavioral black-box interrogation represents a complementary approach to white-box methods such as representation probing \cite{zou2023representation} and contrast-consistent search \cite{burns2022discovering}, applicable when internal activations are inaccessible. Future work should prioritize real-model validation at scale, improved calibration, cross-model generalization studies, and evaluation on naturalistic deception scenarios where models are not explicitly instructed to lie.
