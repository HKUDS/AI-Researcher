\section{Conclusion}

We used the ADAGE adaptive interrogation pipeline to systematically evaluate where and why black-box behavioral probing succeeds or fails at detecting instructed deception in LLMs. The primary contribution is three reusable methodological controls --- prompt equalization, cross-family extraction, and surface-level baselines --- and the empirical findings they enable.

Our central finding is that behavioral detection under controlled conditions is largely an instruction-following artifact, but with a meaningful scale-dependent residual. Removing instruction asymmetry (prompt-equalized control) drops classification accuracy from 93.9--100\% to 52--69\% under independent cross-family extraction, and 61--84\% under same-family (Claude) extraction. The cross-family-extracted signal grows monotonically: 52\% $\to$ 62\% $\to$ 69\% under Mistral Large extraction; same-family extraction yields 61\% (3B) $\to$ 65\% (8B) $\to$ 71\% (7B) $\to$ 82.5\% (14B) $\to$ 84\% (70B), with the largest jump between 7B and 14B (+11.5\,pp). An instructed-matched control on three models (3B, 14B, 70B) decomposes the gap: instruction-following contributes $+$7.5--29\,pp, the dominant confound at all three scales. Multi-turn interrogation is marginal under instructed conditions (+1--3\,pp) but essential under equalization: the $K=1$-to-adaptive gap grows from 0\,pp (3B) to +21.5\,pp (70B), indicating that multi-turn probing adds genuine value only when the instruction-following shortcut is removed. However, a hedging-word regex baseline achieves 87--91\% on models $\geq$14B equalized data, indicating that the detectable signal remains surface-level even at scale.

Three additional findings sharpen this characterization. First, Claude Haiku's 97.8\% LOO accuracy under instructed conditions is driven by 100\% refusal in lying trials---the classifier detects RLHF safety compliance. A full prompt-equalized evaluation ($n=99$) confirms: under neutral prompts, Haiku \emph{corrects} false claims rather than refusing, and LOO drops to 82.8\% (CI [74.7\%, 89.9\%], within the equalized range), confirming that the instructed result was primarily compliance detection. Second, cross-family extraction using two independent extractors (Mistral Large~3 and Qwen~2.5~14B) confirms the 3B$\to$70B scale trend under both, with accuracy 9--16\,pp lower than Claude extraction; however, Qwen breaks monotonicity at 7B, indicating extractor sensitivity. Third, a simple hedging-word regex baseline achieves 86--94\% LOO on instructed data, narrowing the gap to the full LLM-extracted pipeline to 4--14\,pp. Held-out evaluation (50/50 splits, 100 seeds) and permutation tests ($p<0.001$, all models) confirm LOO results are genuine, not overfitting artifacts.

The three controls we introduce --- prompt equalization, cross-family extraction, and hedging baselines --- are not novel in isolation (matched controls, cross-evaluator checks, and simple baselines are standard evaluation methodology), but their systematic application to behavioral deception detection exposes confounds of surprising magnitude: 30\,pp accuracy collapse under equalization, 7$\times$ defensiveness inflation under same-family extraction. No prior behavioral detection work controlled for these effects. The monotonic scale trend (52--69\% cross-family-extracted; 61--84\% same-family-extracted) identifies a genuine knowledge-conflict signal that grows with model capability, with the largest jump between 7B and 14B (+11.5\,pp). Future work should develop knowledge-probing approaches that leverage this scale-dependent correction behavior, use cross-family extractors by default, exploit multi-turn probing under equalized conditions where it provides up to +21.5\,pp over single-question baselines, and evaluate under naturalistic rather than instructed deception scenarios.
