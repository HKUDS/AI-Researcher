\section{Introduction}

Can an automated system determine whether a language model is being deceptive by observing its behavioral responses to strategic questioning? This paper investigates that question empirically and finds that the answer is largely negative: under controlled conditions, behavioral probing primarily detects instruction-following artifacts rather than deceptive behavior per se.

\subsection{Motivation and Scope}

Detecting deception in LLM outputs is a natural goal for AI safety \cite{hubinger2019risks, park2023ai}. While interpretability methods can probe internal representations when model weights are available \cite{elhage2022toy, zou2023representation}, many deployment scenarios are black-box: only the model's textual outputs are observable. This motivates behavioral approaches that operate on model outputs alone. We investigate whether multi-turn behavioral interrogation --- posing strategically sequenced questions and analyzing response patterns --- can distinguish truth-telling from deception-defending LLMs.

We study \emph{instructed roleplay deception} (models prompted to defend a false claim), not autonomous strategic deception. The instructed setting provides a controlled experimental framework with known ground truth. The primary contribution of this paper is not the ADAGE pipeline itself, but rather three reusable methodological controls and the empirical findings they enable:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prompt equalization}: removing instruction asymmetry to isolate knowledge-conflict signal from instruction-following artifacts.
    \item \textbf{Cross-family extraction}: using feature extractors from a different model family than the target to expose extractor-specific scoring effects (including same-family bias and capability differences).
    \item \textbf{Surface-level baselines}: hedging-word regex classifiers that bound the contribution of LLM-extracted features from above.
\end{enumerate}
These controls reveal that reported behavioral detection accuracies are dominated by instruction-following confounds, and that the residual signal, while genuine and scale-dependent, is captured by simple lexical heuristics. Any future behavioral LLM evaluation should adopt these controls to separate genuine detection signal from artifacts.

\subsection{Approach}

The adaptive interrogation framework (ADAGE) serves as a vehicle for systematic experimentation. It comprises: (1)~an LLM-based question generator that produces probing questions conditioned on dialogue history; (2)~a feature extraction pipeline that scores five behavioral dimensions (consistency, specificity, defensiveness, confidence, elaboration) per response; (3)~a confidence-based adaptive stopping mechanism that terminates interrogation early when the classifier is confident (reducing average questions from 8 to 2--3); and (4)~a logistic regression classifier that produces truth/deception predictions with confidence estimates. The contribution lies in the empirical findings enabled by this pipeline, not in the pipeline architecture. The pipeline's strongest empirical justification is in the equalized setting, where multi-turn probing unlocks a scale-dependent signal that single-question baselines miss (the $K=1$-to-adaptive gap reaches $+$21.5\,pp on 70B; Section~\ref{sec:interrogation_role}).

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

Our experiments span mock agents ($n=100$), seven real LLMs across three families (Llama~3.2~3B, Llama~3.1~8B, Mistral~7B, Qwen~2.5~7B, Qwen~2.5~14B, Llama~3.3~70B, Claude Haiku~4.5; $n=50$--$100$ each), and several controls. The key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Behavioral detection is primarily an instruction-following artifact.} LOO-recalibrated accuracy reaches 93.9--100\% under instructed conditions, but a prompt-equalized control (identical instructions, behavioral differences from knowledge-state conflict only) drops this to 52--69\% under independent cross-family extraction (61--84\% under same-family extraction, reflecting both same-family bias and extractor capability differences). The instructed pipeline primarily detects instruction-following mode differences. An instructed-matched control on five models (3B, 8B, 7B, 14B, 70B) decomposes the gap: instruction-following contributes $+$7.5--31\,pp (range across five models), the dominant confound at all five scales, though on the three smaller models ($\leq$8B) this estimate conflates instruction-following with knowledge transfer (Section~5).
    \item \textbf{A genuine knowledge-conflict signal exists and grows with scale.} Under independent cross-family extraction, equalized accuracy improves monotonically: 52\% (3B) $\to$ 62\% (7B) $\to$ 69\% (70B) under Mistral Large extraction. Claude extraction yields 61\% (3B) $\to$ 65\% (8B) $\to$ 71\% (Mistral~7B) $\to$ 74\% (Qwen~7B) $\to$ 82.5\% (14B) $\to$ 84\% (70B) across seven models, but this is 9--16\,pp higher than cross-family extraction (range across three target models), reflecting both same-family bias (where applicable) and extractor capability differences. The largest gain occurs between 7B and 14B (+11.5\,pp under Claude extraction), though the $\leq$7B and $\geq$14B bins differ in family composition as well as scale; within-family controls are needed to fully disentangle these factors (Section~5). A hedging-word regex baseline matches or exceeds the LLM pipeline on all equalized conditions, indicating the signal remains surface-level.
    \item \textbf{Extractor choice substantially affects detection accuracy.} Cross-family extraction (Mistral scoring Claude Haiku transcripts) reduces defensiveness $d$ from $+4.90$ to $+0.63$ and LOO from 97.8\% to 82.6\%, demonstrating same-family bias in the Claude-on-Claude setting. Re-extraction on equalized datasets using two independent extractors (Mistral Large~3 and Qwen~2.5~14B) confirms the 3B$\to$70B trend under both (52\% $\to$ 69\% Mistral; 61\% $\to$ 68\% Qwen), with accuracy 9--16\,pp lower than Claude extraction. The gap reflects both same-family bias (where applicable) and extractor capability differences: Claude assigns higher defensiveness scores to \emph{all} targets, not just Claude targets (Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}). Qwen extraction breaks monotonicity at 7B, indicating extractor sensitivity.
    \item \textbf{RLHF safety compliance confounds capable-model detection.} Claude Haiku's 97.8\% LOO accuracy is driven by 100\% refusal rate in lying trials (72.3\% of truthful trials also contain refusal-marker language, though the extractor correctly assigns low defensiveness; Section~\ref{sec:haiku_defensiveness}). The classifier detects safety compliance, not deception.
    \item \textbf{Simple heuristics capture most of the signal.} A hedging-word regex baseline achieves 86--94\% LOO on instructed data, narrowing the gap to the full LLM-extracted pipeline (94--100\%) to 4--14\,pp.
    \item \textbf{Scale-dependent error inversion}: small models produce false positives (truth classified as deceptive); capable models produce false negatives (convincing deception). Llama~3.3~70B confirms the trend with a balanced error profile intermediate between 3B/7B and Claude Haiku.
    \item \textbf{Multi-turn interrogation is essential under equalization.} Under instructed conditions, a single question ($K=1$) achieves 90.8--99\% LOO, within 1--3\,pp of the full adaptive system. Under equalization, the $K=1$-to-adaptive gap scales from 0\,pp at 3B to +21.5\,pp at 70B, though Qwen~7B is an exception ($-$3\,pp, likely noise at $n=100$). The trend holds across four of five models: 0\,pp (3B), +8\,pp (Mistral~7B), +19.6\,pp (14B), +21.5\,pp (70B)---multi-turn probing adds genuine evidential value only when the instruction-following shortcut is unavailable.
\end{enumerate}

We present this as a methodology-driven evaluation study characterizing the limits of behavioral deception detection---establishing reusable evaluation controls alongside a detailed map of what works, what fails, and what confounds the signal---rather than as a detection framework or a solution to LLM deception detection.
