\section{Introduction}

Can an automated system determine whether a language model is lying by observing its behavioral responses to strategic questioning? This paper investigates that question empirically and finds that the answer is largely negative: under controlled conditions, behavioral probing primarily detects instruction-following artifacts rather than deceptive behavior per se.

\subsection{Motivation and Scope}

Detecting deception in LLM outputs is a natural goal for AI safety \cite{hubinger2019risks, park2023ai}. While interpretability methods can probe internal representations when model weights are available \cite{elhage2022toy, zou2023representation}, many deployment scenarios are black-box: only the model's textual outputs are observable. This motivates behavioral approaches that operate on model outputs alone. We investigate whether multi-turn behavioral interrogation --- posing strategically sequenced questions and analyzing response patterns --- can distinguish truth-telling from lie-defending LLMs.

We study \emph{instructed roleplay deception} (models prompted to defend a false claim), not autonomous strategic deception. The instructed setting provides a controlled experimental framework with known ground truth. Our central contribution is characterizing the limits of this approach: what behavioral detection actually detects, what confounds it, and where it fails.

\subsection{Approach}

ADAGE is an adaptive interrogation framework comprising: (1)~an LLM-based question generator that produces probing questions conditioned on dialogue history; (2)~a feature extraction pipeline that scores five behavioral dimensions (consistency, specificity, defensiveness, confidence, elaboration) per response; (3)~a confidence-based adaptive stopping mechanism that terminates interrogation early when the classifier is confident (reducing average questions from 8 to 2--3); and (4)~a logistic regression classifier that produces truth/lie predictions with confidence estimates.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

Our experiments span mock agents ($n=100$), three real LLMs (Llama~3.2~3B, Mistral~7B, Claude Haiku~4.5; $n=92$--$100$ each), and several controls. The key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Instructed-condition detection works but is confounded.} LOO-recalibrated accuracy reaches 93.9--100\% on real LLMs, but a prompt-equalized control (identical instructions, behavioral differences from knowledge-state conflict only) drops this to 64--71\% across two model families. The instructed pipeline primarily detects instruction-following mode differences.
    \item \textbf{Knowledge-conflict detection is weak and fragile.} Under equalization, all five features collapse ($|d|<1.2$); the 64--71\% LOO recovery rests on a single feature (defensiveness on Mistral; no dominant feature on Llama) with high variance ($\pm$9\,pp).
    \item \textbf{RLHF safety compliance confounds capable-model detection.} Claude Haiku's 97.8\% LOO accuracy is driven by 100\% refusal rate in lying trials. The classifier detects safety compliance, not deception.
    \item \textbf{Simple heuristics capture most of the signal.} A hedging-word regex baseline achieves 86--94\% LOO on instructed data, narrowing the gap to the full LLM-extracted pipeline (94--100\%) to 4--14\,pp.
    \item \textbf{Cross-family extraction reveals same-family inflation; held-out evaluation validates real signal.} Cross-family extraction (Mistral scoring all 92 Claude Haiku transcripts, EXP-A) shows defensiveness $d$ dropping from $+4.90$ to $+0.63$ and LOO falling from 97.8\% to 82.6\%---confirming same-family inflation while the residual above the equalized range (64--71\%) reflects RLHF-refusal language visible to any extractor. LOO accuracy is further validated by 50/50 held-out splits (within 1.3\,pp of LOO) and survives permutation testing ($p<0.001$ all models).
    \item \textbf{Scale-dependent error inversion}: small models produce false positives (truth classified as lying); capable models produce false negatives (convincing lies).
\end{enumerate}

We present ADAGE as an empirical characterization of the limits of instructed lie detection --- mapping what works, what fails, and what confounds the signal --- rather than as a solution to LLM deception detection.
